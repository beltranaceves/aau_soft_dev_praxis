# **Summary of “Review and Critique of the Information Systems Development Project Failure Literature: An Argument for Exploring Information Systems Development Project Distress”**

**Authors:** Zeinab Baghizadeh, Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic, Daniel Schlagwein

## **1. Purpose of the Paper**

The authors review and critique decades of research on **Information Systems Development (ISD) project failure**, arguing that the field is too focused on *failure as a final outcome*. Instead, they propose shifting attention to **ISD project distress**—the problematic, harmful conditions that arise during a project before outright failure occurs.

They also introduce a **multilevel sensemaking framework**, drawn from Weick (1995), to better understand how project distress emerges and unfolds.

The authors argue that existing perspectives fail to capture the *dynamic, complex, and evolving nature* of ISD projects. Most studies explain *why a project failed* instead of *how trouble emerges* and how it might be addressed earlier.

## **3. Review of Existing Research Perspectives**

The paper categorizes ISD failure literature into **three main perspectives**, each with strengths but significant limitations.

### **A. Rationalist Perspective**

*   Treats failure as an *objective, measurable end state* (e.g., over budget, late, missing requirements).
*   Focuses on identifying causal *factors* (organizational, technical, managerial).
*   Criticized for:
    *   oversimplified linear models,
    *   lack of context sensitivity,
    *   limited practical actionability.

### **B. Process Perspective**

*   Views projects as **dynamic sociotechnical processes**.
*   Failure results from process flaws, organizational dynamics, and political interactions.
*   Offers richer insight into project dynamics but often:
    *   still treats failure as a final, irreversible state,
    *   assumes “objective” flaws.

### **C. Narrative / Social Constructivist Perspectives**

*   Emphasize **subjective interpretation**: failure and success are socially constructed.
*   Focus on stories, discourses, and meaning-making among stakeholders.
*   Criticized for:
    *   lack of generalizability,
    *   limited practical guidance,
    *   underplaying technological issues.

***

## **4. Major Gap Identified Across All Perspectives**

All three perspectives treat project failure as an **end state** and neglect:

*   *ongoing problematic situations*,
*   *the emergence and evolution of trouble*,
*   *how actors interpret and respond to early signs*.

This leads to limited insights for practitioners who must detect and respond to problems *before* they cause failure.

***

## **5. Proposed New Focus: ISD Project Distress**

### **Definition:**

> *A harmful and dynamic project condition involving critical problems that can’t be routinely resolved and require careful attention and rapid response.*

Key characteristics:

*   dynamic, fluid, evolving constellation of issues
*   not always easily identifiable
*   not necessarily leading to failure (if addressed early)
*   can escalate if ignored

Examples include:

*   persistent user–developer misunderstandings
*   governance mismatches
*   cultural misfits
*   project management reporting issues
*   breakdowns in team coordination

The authors argue that recognizing and understanding distress could enable **early intervention** and better project outcomes.

***

## **6. The Multilevel Sensemaking Framework**

Drawn from Weick (1995), this framework explains how different actors interpret project situations and how misalignments can produce distress.

### **Levels of Sensemaking:**

#### **1. Intra-project sensemaking**

*   Individual and team-level interpretation of goals, requirements, tasks.
*   Sources of distress:
    *   miscommunication,
    *   conflicting expectations,
    *   team coordination failures.

#### **2. Project management sensemaking**

*   Where project team and governance structures intersect.
*   Sources of distress:
    *   pressure to meet budget/time vs. actual complexity,
    *   inaccurate reporting,
    *   inconsistent stakeholder expectations.

#### **3. Extra-project sensemaking**

*   Organizational and cultural context.
*   Sources of distress:
    *   rigid governance structures,
    *   cultural resistance to new systems,
    *   strategic misalignment between IS and business objectives.

This multilevel model enables researchers to study *where* distress originates and how it evolves.

***

## **7. Research Agenda Proposed**

The authors outline future research questions across the three sensemaking levels, such as:

*   How do team members interpret emerging conflicts?
*   How do managers navigate conflicting stakeholder demands?
*   How do governance and organizational culture contribute to distress?
*   What interventions help detect and alleviate distress early?

This agenda encourages context-rich, process-oriented studies.

***

## **8. Contributions of the Paper**

1.  **Critical review** of ISD failure literature and identification of limitations across three major research streams.
2.  **Introduction of the concept of ISD project distress** as a needed extension of ISD failure research.
3.  **Development of a multilevel sensemaking framework** to study how distress arises and unfolds.
4.  **Proposal of a comprehensive research agenda** to advance the field.

***

## **9. Overall Significance**

The paper urges the IS research community to:

*   shift from retrospective failure analysis to proactive distress detection,
*   focus on early warning signs and intervention possibilities,
*   embrace more nuanced, dynamic, and contextual understanding of ISD projects.

This shift aligns with modern agile and continuous-development project environments, where there may be no clear “end state.”

# **Summary of the Paper**

## **Purpose of the Study**

The paper addresses a longstanding problem: **most IT projects fail to deliver the benefits organisations expect**, even when they are delivered on time, on budget, and to specification. Traditional success factors (e.g., senior management support, user involvement) have not meaningfully improved benefit delivery.

The authors argue that **success should be defined by business benefits realised**, not by the mere completion of a technical artefact.

Their goal is to examine **which factors help organisations actually realise benefits**, especially when using a *benefits realisation management (BRM)* approach.

***

# **Research Approach**

The authors conducted **three in-depth case studies** in UK public-sector organisations, each running multiple IT-enabled change projects:

1.  **Organisation A** – a Strategic Health Authority (NHS)
2.  **Organisation B** – a university
3.  **Organisation C** – a city council

Only **Organisation C** successfully realised meaningful benefits. Comparing it with the other two allowed the authors to identify what made the difference.

***

# **Key Findings: Six Traditional Success Factors Recast for Benefits Realisation**

The authors show that well-known IS success factors must be **reframed** if the goal is business benefits rather than technical delivery.

***

## **1. From Goal Setting → to Detailed Benefits Planning**

*   Traditional: define goals and objectives for the system.
*   Benefits-oriented: explicitly define *measurable benefits*, map them to required business changes, assign ownership, and plan how they will be achieved.

**Findings**

*   Organisation A used benefits templates mechanically; benefits weren’t meaningful or linked to action.
*   Organisation B listed vague benefits but never planned how to achieve them.
*   Organisation C had **“benefit roadmaps”** and clear business-driven plans linking benefits to organisational change.

***

## **2. From Project Management → to Managing Business Transformation**

*   Traditional: manage tasks, milestones, scope, and risks to deliver an artefact.
*   Benefits-oriented: manage *organisational change* over the system’s entire lifecycle.

**Findings**

*   A and B relied heavily on PRINCE2 and traditional project management, with little capacity for organisational change.
*   C explicitly framed projects as **transformation initiatives**, not IT implementations, recalibrating processes, culture, and training.

***

## **3. From Balanced Project Teams → to Coherent Governance Structures**

*   Traditional: cross-functional project teams.
*   Benefits-oriented: governance structures that **empower teams**, align decisions, and ensure benefits-focused oversight across the portfolio.

**Findings**

*   A and B had fragmented governance, political complications, and unclear decision rights.
*   C built **tailored governance structures**, including Vision Groups, Operational Representative Groups, Project Boards, and a Transformation Programme Board.

***

## **4. From Senior Management Support → to Active Business Leadership**

*   Traditional: provide resources and endorsement.
*   Benefits-oriented: *active leadership*, setting vision, mediating competing interests, and driving organisational change.

**Findings**

*   A and B’s leaders delegated involvement to IT managers, seeing IT as “too technical.”
*   C’s senior executives were deeply involved, providing daily guidance, removing barriers, and championing the benefits.

***

## **5. From User Participation → to Stakeholder-Enabled Benefits Realisation**

*   Traditional: users help define requirements and test systems.
*   Benefits-oriented: stakeholders define benefits, shape organisational change, and are empowered to make decisions.

**Findings**

*   A and B had limited, often tokenistic user involvement.
*   C engaged users and even **customers** directly, involving them early, giving them ownership, and empowering them to participate in benefit delivery.

***

## **6. From Rigorous Testing → to Ongoing Benefits Review**

*   Traditional: test the system before go-live.
*   Benefits-oriented: continuously review benefits after implementation, iterate, and adapt.

**Findings**

*   A and B could not even reach reliable operations, preventing benefits review.
*   C conducted **multi-year iterative benefits reviews**, learning and improving through each phase (e.g., CRM project over seven years).

***

# **Cross-Case Conclusion**

Organisation C succeeded because it treated IT as **an enabler of organisational transformation**, not a technical project.  
Key differences:

*   Strong benefit planning
*   Continuous organisational change management
*   Adaptive governance
*   Active senior leadership
*   Deep stakeholder involvement
*   Persistent benefits review and organisational learning

***

# **Six Overarching Principles for Benefits-Oriented Success Factors**

The authors synthesise their findings into general principles:

1.  **Benefits orientation**: success is measured by business outcomes.
2.  **Organisational change focus**: benefits require process, cultural, and structural transformation.
3.  **Context tailoring**: no universal formula; factors must be adapted.
4.  **Interdependence of factors**: success factors amplify each other and cannot be treated as isolated.
5.  **Lifecycle perspective**: benefits emerge long after go-live.
6.  **Portfolio perspective**: manage benefits across all IT initiatives collectively.

***

# **Overall Contribution**

The paper argues that **traditional IS success factors are insufficient** because they focus on technical implementation rather than benefits.  
By reconnecting IS success factors with BRM practices, the authors offer a framework for **how organisations can consistently translate IT investment into value**.
