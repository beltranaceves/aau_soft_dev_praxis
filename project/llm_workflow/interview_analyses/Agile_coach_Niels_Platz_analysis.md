## 1. Methodological Reflection (Patton, 2002)

### 1.1 Interview Type
The Niels Platz interview lines up best with an **Interview Guide Approach** (Patton's Type 2). Interviewers came with topics prepared — agile adoption, power dynamics, management tensions, concrete cases. Questions were shaped as the talk moved forward. The conversation ran its own course.

Here is the evidence.

The interviewers said it plainly: *"It is an informal interview, so it is not that structured. We would like you to go down the road with what you feel is interesting for us to know."*

Questions got adapted on the fly. When Niels brought up management tensions, the follow-up dug deeper. Nobody reached for a script. A break mid-interview gave everyone room to figure out what to ask next. That is classic guide-based work.

**Strength:** Flexibility let Niels speak freely. The data is rich, unfiltered, full of organizational dynamics a rigid protocol would have crushed.

**Limitation:** Steering toward concrete cases was a struggle. Some topics came late or incomplete. The group admitted it: *"The only thing I was hoping to get is something concrete."*

### 1.2 Question Types Used (Patton's Six Types)
The interviewers mixed their question types.

| Question Type | Examples from Interview | Assessment |
|--------------|------------------------|------------|
| **Experience/Behavior** | *"Can you remember any cases where a team could not embrace or adopt some of the Agile work methodologies?"* | Strong. Open-ended. Pulls out narrative. |
| **Opinion/Values** | *"Would you say it starts from the bottom up, from the top down, or from the middle out?"* | Decent. Slightly leading — provides categories. |
| **Feeling** | *"What tensions in particular did you experience?"* | Implicit feeling question. Could have pushed emotional responses further. |
| **Knowledge** | Few pure knowledge questions present. The interview treats Niels as an expert. That is fair. | Adequate. |
| **Sensory** | Not used. | A loss. "What do you see walking into a Q Planning session?" would have hit differently. |
| **Background/Demographic** | Taken at the start: education, career history. | Well placed. |

**Question quality assessment:**
Most questions were **truly open-ended**: *"What tensions...?"*, *"Can you remember any cases...?"*
One near-**dichotomous** question stood out: *"Bottom-up, top-down, or middle-out?"* Niels refused the box. He answered: *"You need to do all of them."*
Some questions were **singular and clear**. That is good practice.
**Probing** worked well sometimes — the follow-up on management tensions was solid. Deeper dives into specific stories would have yielded even more.

### 1.3 Sequencing (Patton's Guidance)
Patton says: present behaviors first. Then opinions and feelings. Then past and future. Demographics at the end.

The interview roughly holds this shape.
1. **Opening:** Background and demographics. Establishes context. Works.
2. **Early:** Present behaviors of agile adoption at Norlys.
3. **Middle:** Opinions on management tensions, power dynamics.
4. **Later:** Concrete cases from the past. Future vision (One 2027).
5. **Closing:** Future-oriented reflections.

Reasonable sequencing. Patton would still put demographics at the tail end.

## 2. Thematic Coding & Theory Mapping

### 2.1 Theme: Management as the Bottleneck

**Summary:** One theme stomps all others. Niels keeps circling back to management. Middle management especially. It is the hardest piece of the whole puzzle.

**Key evidence:**
Niels said it straight: *"Management is the hard part, because they need to do things differently."*
He pushed further: *"The mindset up there is super hard to change."*
And: *"Middle management is maybe the hardest place to change."*
Then: *"Management is the strongest tool we have. If we enable it, it can contaminate the rest."*
One line cuts deepest: *"When you move up into the managerial layer, you reach more political ambitions. That means there are agendas you cannot see. There are power struggles."*

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Mintzberg** | Middle line resists change. Strategic apex (IT director Christian Nord) enables. Technostructure pulls to rationalize. Operating core pulls to professionalize. |
| **Management Fashion** | Old management habits block mindful adoption. Some managers dabble in agile ceremonies without real change. |
| **Weinberg's Overstructured Management** | DEAL model applies. Managers blame external factors instead of changing their own behavior. Sequence fallacy: they want fixed plans. |
| **Paradox Lens** | Management control vs. team autonomy. The classic structural tension. |
| **Four Paradigms** | Functionalism (management as expert, objective reality) crashes against Social Relativism (agile as consensus, socially built). |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"The first part we needed to do was to stop the bleeding. When you talk about Agile, it is nice to write about and read about, and teams are actually the easy ones. Management is the hard part."*

### 2.2 Theme: Evolution, Not Revolution

**Summary:** Norlys picked the slow road on purpose. Evolutionary change. Imperfect intermediate states accepted. No radical restructuring.

**Key evidence:**
Niels: *"We started with behavior: trying to create a common agreement of what we are working on."*
On the two paths: *"You can take revolutions... or you can take evolutions, which is what we are doing here."*
Risk shapes the choice: *"Evolutions have less risk because they still allow you to deliver value during the transition."*
Imperfect tools get used openly: *"We sometimes put in things that do not make sense. For instance, the TPM role is an antipattern of huge proportions, but it is a stepping stone toward something better."*
Q Planning falls in the same category: *"Q Planning is, from my point of view, a necessity where we are right now. But it is not something I want in the future."*

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Management Fashion** | **Strongest fit.** This is textbook **Tailor** strategy (Cram & Newell's most mindful bucket). Aware current practices are flawed. Adapting deliberately. Planning the next move. |
| **Paradox Lens** | Tensions accepted as built-in. "Stepping stones" become temporary paradox-management tools. |
| **Mintzberg** | Structural change moves gradually. The org sits between configurations. |
| **Weinberg's Overstructured Management** | Direct contrast to the Sequence Fallacy. Niels throws out the idea of a fixed transformation sequence. |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"So we actually started with behavior: trying to create a common agreement of what we are working on. By starting with changing the way we work... you make the basis for doing an organizational transformation afterwards."*

### 2.3 Theme: Power Dynamics & Middle Management Resistance

**Summary:** Command-and-control died. Servant leadership was supposed to replace it. That shift broke middle managers. Some reshaped themselves. Others got shown the door.

**Key evidence:**
Niels painted the picture: *"They are going from being an authority, the one deciding... to suddenly realizing: what is my new role? Am I actually a PM, or am I a line manager? This is super hard for people to realize."*
Education came first: *"We have educated our line managers... Some of them felt that not everybody is going to stay here because of this."*
The shake-up was real: *"Part of the management was changed. They have done that quite a lot, which is not healthy for any company, but it is part of a hiring and firing strategy around this."*
The approach stayed patient: *"You need to prove what you do in parallel with working with them and explaining why it works."*

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Mintzberg** | Middle line loses ground to operating core. Strategic apex pushes change. Technostructure (finance, compliance) still pulls the other way. |
| **Weinberg's Overstructured Management** | Modularization Fallacy: label someone "line manager" and expect the new role to click. Choice Fallacy: old role vs. new role treated as binary. |
| **Four Paradigms** | Radical Structuralist lens: management as a class protecting its turf. Neohumanist lens: power blocks rational conversation. |
| **Paradox Lens** | Management control vs. team autonomy. Negotiated in real time. |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"They are going from being an authority, the one deciding, the one who understood the business needs, to suddenly realizing: what is my new role?"*

### 2.4 Theme: Team Maturity Variation

**Summary:** Teams at Norlys live on completely different maturity levels. Niels adjusts his coaching to match. This is mindful work. Situational. Never one-size-fits-all.

**Key evidence:**
One team needs story points explained. Another is asking about innovation sprints. Niels put it plainly: *"Some teams are good at it. We can give more free rein there. Some teams need to be held in the hand a bit more."*
He explained why: *"We cannot just give them free rein, because they do not know what to do with it yet."*
The judgment call is constant: *"You need to look at what level they are on, where they are maturity-wise, where a change will give most value, and where you would cause the most damage by changing that first."*

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Mintzberg** | Different teams can sit in different configurations at once. An organizational hybrid. |
| **Weinberg's Cultural Patterns** | **Strong fit.** Teams spread across maturity levels. Some at Pattern 1 (Variable). Some nearing Pattern 3 (Steering). |
| **Management Fashion** | Tailoring to context. The opposite of a uniform rollout. |
| **Paradox Lens** | Homogeneity vs. heterogeneity. Norlys lets heterogeneity stand. That is mature and adaptive. |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"We need to be aware that each change needs to give value, not just break things. Otherwise they need too long to catch up, and then they will again just fit into whatever box you told them to stay in."*

### 2.5 Theme: Stepping Stones & Antipatterns as Deliberate Strategy

**Summary:** Norlys knowingly picks up flawed practices. Antipatterns. Temporary stepping stones. This is not naive. It is sophisticated change management.

**Key evidence:**

| **Paradox Lens** | Stepping stones handle paradoxical tension. Accept suboptimal now. Enable progress later. |
| **Weinberg's Overstructured Management** | Contrast to Sequence Fallacy. Niels does not follow a fixed path. He adapts to what works. |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**

### 2.6 Theme: Transparency as Prerequisite

**Summary:** Agile practices could only take root after Norlys built transparency from scratch. It did not exist before.

**Key evidence:**
Niels named it early: *"One of the risks we had up front was that we first needed to create transparency in a company where it did not exist."*
The old structure was fragmented: *"Different smaller silos were doing different things."*
He repeated the point: *"Transparency is key, and that is also in SAFe."*
The cost of opacity was concrete: *"We did not have teams that did not wait for other teams. That means code starts to smell, interfaces change, customer needs change, and code is forgotten."*

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Mintzberg** | Silos as functional grouping. Norlys shifts toward market and product grouping. Q Planning becomes a liaison device for transparency. |
| **Control** | Transparency unlocks both formal and informal control. Shared backlogs let you measure without heavy bureaucracy. |
| **Hyper-learning** | Transparency feeds distributed gate-keeping and peer networks. Knowledge cannot move through silos. |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"We first needed to create transparency in a company where it did not exist."*

### 2.7 Theme: The Story Points Tension

**Summary:** Story points became a battlefield. Agile principles fought management and finance needs. The surprising twist: external pressure ended up driving real internal adoption.

**Key evidence:**
Finance pushed hard: *"Finance has asked about this... every story, before it is closed, should have story points and an application connected to it, so they could know cost."*
Some teams had their own system: *"Some were estimating in hours... This guy had an error rate on hours of less than six percent."*
The contradiction was clear: *"Adding story points at the end is not Agile methodology."*
Then something shifted. The team *"could not find any poker planning cards, so we created our own."* Resistance turned into voluntary adoption.

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Weinberg's Overstructured Management** | Iteration Fallacy: "story points" risk becoming a cliché. But metrics can also be translated across levels. |
| **Management Fashion** | Authentic agile practices (relative estimation) clash with ceremonial compliance (adding points at the end for finance). |
| **Paradox Lens** | Efficiency vs. innovativeness. Finance wants predictability. Teams want flexible estimation. |
| **Control** | Formal control (finance demands) sits alongside informal control (teams adopt the practice themselves). |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"Adding story points at the end is not Agile methodology. But having the talk with this guy resulted in him writing to me less than a week later: 'Now all my three teams are estimating in story points. They could not find any poker planning cards, so we created our own.'"*

### 2.8 Theme: The Scrum Master Case (Removal & Reinstatement)

**Summary:** A new manager walked in and axed all Scrum Masters from the platform teams. Direction collapsed. The manager got removed too. Hard evidence that agile roles carry real weight.

**Key evidence:**
Niels laid out the timeline: *"Our platform teams... started as one team... then they evolved. Now they are four teams."*
Then: *"Then a new manager came in and fired all the Scrum Masters, and things stopped."*
The result: *"They created a new application called the [Guba Super Tool], but they did not know what their customers wanted."*
The ending: *"That manager has now been removed... they are going to have Scrum Masters again. They saw that it simply costs too much to remove the catalyst in the teams."*

**Theoretical connections:**

| Theory | Connection |
|--------|-----------|
| **Management Fashion** | Dabbler behavior. A manager who did not get agile killed the agile roles. The organization learned from the wreckage. That is mindful. |
| **Hyper-learning** | Loss of gate-keepers. Scrum Masters were knowledge catalysts. Without them, customer connection evaporated. |
| **Mintzberg** | The new manager (middle line) imposed direct supervision. Removed liaison devices. The structure broke. |
| **Weinberg's Overstructured Management** | Modularization Fallacy: Scrum Masters labelled unnecessary without understanding what they did. Sequence Fallacy: assuming teams could run without catalysts. |
| **Control** | Informal control mechanisms vanished. Scrum Masters had been clan builders. Self-organization collapsed. |

**Quote for fieldstone collection:**
> *"They saw that it simply costs too much to remove the catalyst in the teams."*

## 5. Interview Quality & Limitations

### Strengths
1. **Rich, unfiltered responses.** Niels speaks his mind. Vivid metaphors. Concrete cases.
2. **Deep expertise.** Seventeen-plus years of practice. CS degree. A thesis on software project crisis using organizational theory.
3. **Multiple levels covered.** Individual developers. Teams. Management. Strategic One 2027 vision.
4. **Tensions named out loud.** Niels does not clean things up. He calls out conflicts and failures.

### Limitations (for the exam paper)
1. **Single perspective.** One person's view. An Agile coach. Not a line manager. Not a developer. Not a product owner. Valuable. Partial.
2. **No direct observation.** We hear about Q Planning, retrospectives. We do not see them.
3. **Some vagueness.** Names are marked [unclear] in the transcript. Hard to reference cases precisely.
4. **Interviewer inexperience.** Some follow-ups needed more depth. The "concrete case" question arrived late.
5. **Positive bias.** Niels is invested in the transformation. He may soft-pedal failures.
6. **Time constraints.** The interviewers noted *"It should have ended eight minutes ago."* Some topics got squeezed.

### Mitigation Strategies

**On the identity crisis of middle managers:**
> *"They are going from being an authority, the one deciding, the one who understood the business needs, to suddenly realizing: what is my new role?"*

**On deliberate antipatterns:**
> *"We sometimes put in things that do not make sense. For instance, the TPM role is an antipattern of huge proportions, but it is a stepping stone toward something better."*

**On the value of agile catalysts:**
> *"They saw that it simply costs too much to remove the catalyst in the teams."*

**On management as contaminant and enabler:**
> *"Management is the strongest tool we have. If we enable it, it can contaminate the rest."*

**On evolution over revolution:**
> *"Evolutions have less risk because they still allow you to deliver value during the transition. A revolution risks that you die because you did not succeed in your revolution."*

**On developers and standards:**
> *"We create higher standards than what management would demand."*

**On starting with behavior, not structure:**
> *"You can start by doing an organizational transformation, which always messes up, because you start by just putting people in a new box, and they will act as they did before."*

**On the coach's role:**
> *"As Agile coaches, we need to be more extreme, otherwise it will not fall through and they will not see the value."*

**On theory vs. practice:**
> *"Theory is super important, but let it hit the real world, and let the real world have a veto right."*
