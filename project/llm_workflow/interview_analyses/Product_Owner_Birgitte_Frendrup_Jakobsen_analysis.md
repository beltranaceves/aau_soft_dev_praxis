### Q2: "What is one of the most important tasks of your day?"

| Property | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Patton Type** | Opinion/Values |
| **Quality Assessment** | Good follow-up. Slightly leading: *"most important"* presupposes there is one. |
| **Answer Summary** | Scoping demands. Preventing **scope creep**. Business stakeholders struggle to define what they want. They tend to expand requirements during development. Birgitte locks scope, manages expectations, slices deliveries. |
| **Key Quotes** | > *"Sometimes it can be hard for the business to actually define what they want. So when we are trying to scope it, they have a tendency to scope creep. You know, they want more."* |
| **Primary Theory Fit** | PARADOX. Blueprint vs. Continuous Planning. WEIN-MGMT: Choice Fallacy. Scope as binary decision vs. continuous negotiation. |
| **Secondary Theory Fit** | CONTROL. Formal measurement. Scope documents as control artifacts. |
| **Emic Terms** | "Scope creep" |
| **Fieldstone Score** | ★★. Good illustration of the business-development tension. |

**Analysis:**
Scope creep looks like a **behavioral problem** on the surface. Business stakeholders keep asking for more. Through a theoretical lens it is a **structural tension**. The QBR planning model draws a three-month blueprint. Business needs keep moving. Birgitte sits between those forces. She **absorbs the tension**. That is the **Blueprint vs. Continuous Planning** paradox from Iivari.

Weinberg would flag the framing of scope as something to be "locked." That is **Choice Fallacy** territory. Scope treated as a binary: agreed or changed. It is actually a continuous negotiation. To her credit, Birgitte says *"sometimes you have to change a little bit."* She sees the continuum.

### Q4: "How do you see your role today — as a Product Owner or as something else?"

| Property | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Patton Type** | Opinion/Values + Background |
| **Quality Assessment** | Good clarifying question. Shows interviewers adapting to new information. |
| **Answer Summary** | Birgitte is now a **Technical Product Manager (TPM)**. The role does not exist in any Scrum guide. Norlys invented it to help POs in multi-team cases. The job is coordination, answering business questions, finding answers. She expects to be "out of job in about three years" as POs grow into the role. |
| **Key Quotes** | > *"I'm a technical product manager today and it's not something you can find in any Scrum guide at all... I should be out of job in about three years, because what I do is within the product owner role. But because it's very new in Norlys to drive this way on IT development, we have set in the layer."* |
| **Primary Theory Fit** | MANAGEMENT FASHION. **Tailor**. Norlys invents a new role to close the maturity gap. Mindful design. Not ceremonial. |
| **Secondary Theory Fit** | MINTZ. TPM as temporary liaison device. A "stepping stone" toward a more mature configuration. HYPER-LEARNING: TPM as distributed gate-keeper, helping POs learn their role. |
| **Emic Terms** | "Technical Product Manager" |
| **Fieldstone Score** | ★★★. Key quote about role invention and the temporary nature of the TPM position. |

**Analysis:**
This is the richest answer in the interview. The TPM role is a **deliberate organizational invention**. No methodology prescribed it. That is Cram and Newell's **Tailor** category exactly. Norlys does not follow Scrum prescriptively. It builds new structures where maturity gaps exist.

The line *"I should be out of job in about three years"* is striking. The TPM role is explicitly **temporary**. A **stepping stone** (Niels's word) toward a state where POs can operate without this layer. That is highly **mindful** design. A role created with an expiration date.

From **hyper-learning**, Birgitte functions as a **distributed gate-keeper**. Not for technical knowledge. For **procedural and organizational knowledge**. She teaches POs how to navigate the business, scope demands, manage stakeholders. Her success metric: making herself unnecessary.

From **Mintzberg**, the TPM is a **liaison device** inserted into the **middle line** to compensate for low organizational maturity in agile product ownership. As maturity rises, the liaison device dissolves.

### Q6: "Does adaptation happen in just your team, or across the whole company?"

| Property | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Patton Type** | Knowledge |
| **Quality Assessment** | Good follow-up. Probes the scope of change. |
| **Answer Summary** | The adaptation covers the whole process. The whole company had to shift. Business stakeholders had to learn new ways of requesting work. Timelines were new. People were new. Before, stakeholders walked straight to a team. Now they go through a structured process. The goal: optimize resources, give developers focus through 14-day sprints with clear scope. |
| **Key Quotes** | > *"Everything is new. The timeline is new. The people are new, the ones they used to talk to; they would usually just go to a team. And that was very easy and nice for them, not so much for the team, because they were not the only ones who did it. Everybody did it."* |
| **Primary Theory Fit** | MINTZ. Transformation hits all parts at once. Behavioral change across strategic apex, middle line, and operating core. |
| **Secondary Theory Fit** | MANAGEMENT FASHION. Mindful adoption needs organization-wide change. Not just IT. |
| **Emic Terms** | None new |
| **Fieldstone Score** | ★★★. Vivid before-state description. Clear rationale for structured change. |

**Analysis:**
Birgitte's **before-state** picture hits hard. *"They would usually just go to a team."* That is the coordination chaos the QBR model was built to fix. Niels said the same: **transparency as a prerequisite**. Before the structured model, nobody knew what teams were working on.

The phrase *"that was very easy and nice for them, not so much for the team"* names the **fundamental tension**: **business convenience vs. team efficiency**. The old model was comfortable for stakeholders. It wrecked teams with interruptions, context-switching, zero focus. The new model trades stakeholder comfort for team productivity.

Mintzberg would call this a shift from **ad hoc mutual adjustment** (anyone talks to any team) to **standardized coordination** (structured process, clear entry points). Formalization that happens when organizations outgrow informal coordination.

### Q8: "When you see something not working, how do you implement changes — at team level or company level?"

| Property | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Patton Type** | Opinion/Values + Experience |
| **Quality Assessment** | Good. Probes the change implementation process. |
| **Answer Summary** | First priority is always the team. If the team level is broken, nothing gets delivered. She will "move heaven and earth" to fix team issues. Sometimes makes decisions and apologizes afterward. After fixing the team, she escalates the systemic issue up the organization. |
| **Key Quotes** | > *"I think my first priority is always to help the team. So if it's not working in the team, that's the highest priority. It has to work in the team, otherwise we don't deliver. So I would move heaven and earth to get that done. and I sometimes make decisions and then I have to say 'I'm sorry' afterwards to a leader of any kind."* |
| **Primary Theory Fit** | PARADOX. Management Control vs. Team Autonomy. Birgitte picks team over hierarchy, manages the fallout. |
| **Secondary Theory Fit** | WEIN-MGMT. Anti-overstructured. Acts decisively, asks forgiveness later. Does not wait for permission. |
| **Emic Terms** | None new |
| **Fieldstone Score** | ★★★. Reveals her operational philosophy and the real priority order. |

**Analysis:**
This is where Birgitte's **operational philosophy** shows. She puts **team functionality above hierarchical protocol**. *"I sometimes make decisions and then I have to say I'm sorry afterwards."* That is someone who knows rigid process can kill team effectiveness.

Weinberg would call this anti-overstructured management. Birgitte does not let organizational structure stop her. She takes **ownership** (Effort and Ability in DEAL terms). She does not blame the system (Difficulty).

Through the **paradox lens**, this is a pragmatic move on the **Management Control vs. Team Autonomy** tension. When the tension peaks (team is blocked), she **resolves for the team** and deals with the control consequences afterward.

### Q9: "How do you conclude that something doesn't work?"

| Property | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Patton Type** | Experience/Behavior (process) |
| **Quality Assessment** | Good probing follow-up. |
| **Answer Summary** | When a team or PO flags an issue, Birgitte analyzes everything: feature description, her own input, business information, environmental changes. Then she checks recurrence. A one-off? They live with it. A recurring pattern? Systematic change. |
| **Key Quotes** | > *"You also have to think, okay, will this ever happen again? Is this a general problem or is it one we have once a year? Because if it's once a year, then we live with it. Then we don't make a lot of changes to fix it."* |
| **Primary Theory Fit** | WEIN-MGMT. Anti-Iteration Fallacy. She does not apply the same fix to everything. Triage by recurrence. |
| **Secondary Theory Fit** | HYPER-LEARNING. Simple design rule: "Once? Live with it. Recurs? Fix it." |
| **Emic Terms** | None new |
| **Fieldstone Score** | ★. Practical. Minor. |

**Analysis:**
Birgitte's triage rule — *"if it's once a year, we live with it"* — is a **simple design rule** in hyper-learning terms. It's a heuristic that prevents over-investment in rare problems while ensuring systemic issues are addressed. This is pragmatic, not overstructured.
