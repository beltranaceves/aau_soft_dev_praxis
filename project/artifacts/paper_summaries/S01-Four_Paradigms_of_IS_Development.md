# S01: Four Paradigms of Information Systems Development

## Source
Hirschheim, R., & Klein, H. K. (1989). Four paradigms of information systems development. *Communications of the ACM*, 32(10), 1199-1216.

## Core Framework
Based on Burrell & Morgan's (1979) sociological paradigms, mapped onto two dimensions:
1. **Subjectivist ↔ Objectivist** (assumptions about knowledge)
2. **Order ↔ Conflict** (assumptions about social world)

These yield **four paradigms** — each with a developer archetype, story, and consequences.

---

## Paradigm 1: Functionalism — "Analyst as Systems Expert"

### Core Beliefs
- **Ontology**: Realist — objective empirical reality exists independently
- **Epistemology**: Positivist — measurable cause-effect relationships
- **Social view**: Order, integration, consensus, functional coordination
- **Developer role**: Expert applying formal concepts, rational tools, and methods
- **Systems development**: "From without" — planned intervention with rationalistic methods

### Key Concepts
- Requirements are objective, "true requirements" can be specified
- Management determines ends; developer chooses means
- Politics is irrational and interferes with efficiency
- Systems model organizational reality
- Ends = profit maximization, efficiency, effectiveness

### Dysfunctional Consequences
- Focus on means rather than debating ends
- Assumes ends are agreed when they often are not
- Power and conflict are ignored
- Leads to user resistance to change
- Ignores that people "observe back" — observation is not neutral

### Examples
Structured analysis, information engineering, expert systems

---

## Paradigm 2: Social Relativism — "Analyst as Facilitator"

### Core Beliefs
- **Ontology**: Nominalist — reality is socially constructed, product of human mind
- **Epistemology**: Anti-positivist — search for causal explanations is misguided; sense-making instead
- **Social view**: Order (but order is subjective/inter-subjective)
- **Developer role**: Catalyst/facilitator improving subjective understanding and cultural sensitivity
- **Systems development**: "From within" — adapting to internal forces of evolutionary social change

### Key Concepts
- No single reality, only different perceptions
- Ends and means are fluid and reversible
- System objectives emerge through social interaction (sense-making)
- Consensus/acceptance = legitimacy
- Prototyping and evolutionary learning
- Developer is like a midwife — eases the process but doesn't design

### Tools
Diary keeping, mappings (historical, diagnostic, ecological, virtual), group pedagogy, metaphors

### Weaknesses
- Complete relativism — no way to distinguish valid from manipulative consensus
- Uncritical of potential dysfunctional side effects of tools/techniques

### Examples
Ethnographic approaches, FLORENCE project

## Paradigm 3: Radical Structuralism — "Analyst as Labor Partisan"

### Core Beliefs
- **Ontology**: Realist — objective economic reality, class conflict is endemic
- **Epistemology**: Positivist (materialist view of history)
- **Social view**: Conflict — class struggle between owners and labor
- **Developer role**: Warrior/partisan raising ideological consciousness through organized political action
- **Systems development**: "From without" — adapting tools to different social class interests

### Key Concepts
- Fundamental conflict between owners/shareholders and labor
- Management sides with owners
- Developer must choose: side with management (rationalization against workers) or labor (enhance craft, working conditions)
- Technology is NOT socially neutral — it is biased toward capital/management interests
- User resistance is POSITIVE — sign of labor becoming aware of collective interest

### Dysfunctional Consequences
- Activism over interpretation — reduces possibility of justified consensus
- Oversimplifies to two parties
- Ignores conflicts within labor (e.g., between unions)
- Fallacy of historicism — all events seen as inevitable class conflict

### Examples
Trade-union led approaches, UTOPIA project, DEMOS project

## Paradigm 4: Neohumanism — "Analyst as Emancipator or Social Therapist"

### Core Beliefs
- **Ontology**: Dual — realist for technical interests, nominalist for mutual understanding/emancipation
- **Epistemology**: Dual — positivist for technical control, anti-positivist for mutual understanding/emancipation
- **Social view**: Conflict (but conflict can be resolved through rational discourse)
- **Developer role**: Emancipator/social therapist improving human understanding and rationality through emancipation
- **Systems development**: "From within" — improving human understanding, liberating from unwarranted constraints

### Core Concepts: Habermas' Three Knowledge Interests

| Knowledge Interest | Purpose | Domain |
|--------------------|---------|--------|
| **Technical (TKI)** | Control over nature and people | Work — engineering sciences |
| **Mutual Understanding** | Improving understanding of culture, psyche, others | Culture — hermeneutics |
| **Emancipatory** | Establishing truth and justice; removing unwarranted constraints | Freedom — critical social theory |

### Barriers to Rational Discourse
1. **Authority and illegitimate power** — people distort/withhold information
2. **Peer opinion pressure/groupthink** — tunnel vision for loyalty
3. **Time, space, resource limitations** — prevents universal access
4. **Social differentiation** — education, specialization differences cause misunderstanding
5. **Bias and limitation of language use** — jargon, cognitive anchoring

### Key Idea: Ideal Speech Situation
A debate free of social pressure where all arguments are checked for:
- Intelligibility
- Veracity
- Truthfulness
- Appropriateness

### Weaknesses
- Difficult to implement in practice
- Assumes people are willing and able to radically change behavior
- Unclear if there are natural limits to human potential

### Examples
Critical social theory, SAMPO project

## Eight Features Where Paradigms Produce Different Systems

1. **Technology architecture** — how hardware/software matches organizational units
2. **Kind of information flows** — intended meanings of information
3. **Control of users** — how system affects power/authority relationships
4. **Control of systems development** — who controls the development process
5. **Access to information** — who has access and who benefits
6. **Error handling** — errors as sanctions vs learning opportunities
7. **Training** — mechanical skills vs enhancing individual/social position
8. **Raison d'être** — primary reason for the system's existence

## How to Use This in Interview Analysis
1. **Classify stakeholder assumptions**: Categorize each interviewee's statements about systems development into one of the four paradigms
2. **Identify paradigm conflicts**: Look for tensions between stakeholders operating from different paradigmatic assumptions
3. **Trace consequences**: Relate paradigm assumptions to the eight system features above
4. **Analyze power and control**: Use the radical structuralist lens to examine who controls development and whose interests are served
5. **Assess communication barriers**: Use the neohumanist lens to identify barriers to rational discourse in the interviews
6. **Position yourself**: Your own analysis will operate within some paradigm — be explicit about which
