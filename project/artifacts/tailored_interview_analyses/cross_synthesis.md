## 2. The Anchor Case: The Scrum Master Removal

The single most theoretically rich incident in the data is **the Scrum Master removal case**, related by Niels (Q23 of his interview, and surfaced again in the closing reflections on the catalyst function). The case is described in `Niels_Platz_v2.md` § 5.1; this synthesis foregrounds it.

**The case, in brief.**
- The platform teams at Norlys started as one team with a Scrum Master and a product owner. They evolved into four teams, each with its own area.
- A *new manager* (middle line) came in and *fired all the Scrum Masters*.
- Things "stopped." The team built the *Guba Super Tool* without knowing what customers wanted. The new tool was misaligned.
- The new manager has since been *removed*. The teams are getting Scrum Masters back. *"They saw that it simply costs too much to remove the catalyst in the teams."*

**Why it is the anchor.**

| Theory | What the case shows | Code |
|--------|---------------------|------|
| **Weinberg** | The manager treated the Scrum Master as a swappable module | **Anti-Modularization** ✓ |
| **Weinberg** | The manager did not let the real world have a veto right — they imposed a direct supervision on a context that needed catalysts | **Anti-Iteration** ✓ (vicariously) |
| **Lyytinen** | The team lost its *distributed gate-keeper* (DGK) and a node in the *peer network* (PN). Knowledge scanning stopped; customers went unheard. | **DGK** ✓ and **PN** ✓ |
| **Lyytinen** | A *simple design rule* (the Scrum Master as a *catalyst* role) was removed, and the surrounding routines (retrospectives, customer connection) lost their holder. | **SDR** ✓ (vicariously) |
| **Mintzberg** | The middle line (ML) reversed a *Pull to Collaborate* (P-COL) that the support-staff Scrum Master had been enacting | **ML** ✓ and **P-COL reversed** |
| **Mintzberg** | The manager imposed *direct supervision* on a context that operated through *mutual adjustment* | coordination-mechanism shift ✓ |

The case is, in a single narrative, the *behavioural* (Weinberg), the *learning-routine* (Lyytinen), and the *structural* (Mintzberg) evidence for the paper's argument. **The paper's logical chain literally happens in this one story.**

**Why the case carries argumentative weight.** A single incident that maps onto all three theoretical frames is unusual. The paper's structure — Weinberg → Lyytinen → Mintzberg — can be *walked through* the case, step by step. The case therefore lets the paper move from *parallel application of three theories* to *sequential application of three theories on the same data point*, which is more economical and more rhetorically effective.

## 3. The Logical Chain: Fallacies → Routines → Structure

The three theories form a *chain* when read sequentially. For each chain segment, the strongest supporting evidence:

### 3.1 Fallacies → Routines

*Where a manager exhibits a fallacy, what routine is missing or weakened?*

| Manager Fallacy | Missing/Weakened Routine | Evidence |
|-----------------|--------------------------|----------|
| **Modularization** (treated SM as a swappable module) | Loss of DGK + PN. The Scrum Master was the team's *gate-keeper* and *peer-network node*. | Niels's anchor case. |
| **Sequence** (assumed a fixed transformation order) | Weakens SDR. Without stepping stones, the routines that *would* emerge have no foothold. | Niels's "evolutions, not revolutions." Eva-Maria's IP sprint is the *anti-sequence* routine. |
| **Recursion** (one methodology fits all problems) | Weakens PA. Parallel ambidexterity requires *different frames* for exploration and exploitation. | Eva-Maria's "cheat" — she flips the frame mid-sprint. |
| **Iteration** (repeated the same ceremony regardless of feedback) | Weakens PN. Peer networks only work if the network *learns from the ceremonies*. | Kasper's Next.js case — the team *did* learn, *because* the rule allowed it. |
| **Choice** (binary framing) | Weakens DGK. Gate-keeping requires holding multiple frames simultaneously. | Birgitte's 80/20 guardrail is a *non-binary* SDR. |

The chain segment is: *rejection of each fallacy enables a specific routine* (or *each fallacy's embrace weakens a specific routine*). The Scrum Master case is the cleanest illustration.

### 3.2 Routines → Structure

*Which organizational part supports or undermines each routine?*

| Routine | Part that supports it | Pull that supports it | Evidence |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|----------|
| **DGK** | Support Staff (agile coaches, EAs, TPMs) | P-COL | Niels, Rasmus, Birgitte. |
| **BEK** | Strategic Apex (apex enabled the Telia acquisition) | P-TL | Niels, Rasmus. |
| **SDR** | Operating Core (the "picture" rule is the team's, not the apex's) | P-PRO | Kasper, Birgitte. |
| **PN** | Operating Core + Support Staff (peer knowledge-sharing) | P-PRO + P-COL | Kasper, Eva-Maria. |
| **PA** | Operating Core (the IP sprint lives at team level) | P-PRO | Eva-Maria. |

The structural reading: *the operating core, supported by the support staff and (selectively) the apex, is the engine of hyper-learning*. The technostructure (P-RAT) is the *countervailing force* — standardization is necessary but, as the Scrum Master case shows, can be over-applied.

### 3.3 Fallacies → Routines → Structure (combined)

Reading the chain end-to-end:

1. **Weinberg** describes the *failure modes* of managerial thinking. Norlys rejects all five fallacies, with the Modularization Fallacy's rejection demonstrated most vividly by the Scrum Master case.
2. **Lyytinen et al.** describe the *learning routines* that *replace* overstructured thinking. Norlys runs all four routines plus parallel ambidexterity, with the SDR and DGK routines most strongly anchored.
3. **Mintzberg** describes the *structural conditions* under which the routines can survive. Norlys is in transition: P-COL and P-PRO are strong; P-RAT is still active; the middle line is the principal resistance. The structural reading explains *why* the transformation is "incomplete."

The chain maps onto a *single* corporate event (the Scrum Master removal) in the way described in § 2. This is what makes the data unusually tractable for the paper.

## 4. Cross-Interview Patterns

Beyond the anchor case, four cross-cutting patterns emerged:

### 4.1 *Friction* as a load-bearing design choice

Three of the five interviewees describe the *same* design pattern: making the "right" path easy and the "wrong" path costly.
- **Rasmus** (Q12, Q13): *"Using our Kubernetes platform is easy and self-service, while going off-platform requires much more effort and justification."*
- **Kasper** (Q20): *"We have platform teams that work on improving the developer experience… You do not have to configure a Dockerfile or a pipeline."*
- **Niels** (Q21, on what changed when SMs returned): The implicit re-installation of the catalyst role.
- **Birgitte** (Q20): The PO mandate is a *friction* design for the business — easy to enter the backlog with a value story, hard to enter with a "just do it."

This is *Mintzberg P-RAT* and *Lyytinen SDR/DGK* rolled into one design choice. It is a *theoretically dense* pattern that the paper can use to anchor its structural argument.

### 4.2 The "stepping stone" as a unified theoretical artefact

Multiple interviewees describe *temporary, knowingly-flawed practices* that exist for a reason:
- The *TPM role* (Birgitte: "I should be out of a job in three years")
- The *Q Planning event* (Niels: "a necessity where we are right now. But it is not something I want in the future")
- The *IP sprint* (Eva-Maria: "I cheat")
- The *reference framework* (Kasper: a "maturing" IT company)
- The *friction regime* (Rasmus: "We have to give… time later to clean it up")

Each of these is a *Weinberg Anti-Sequence* artefact (no fixed roadmap), a *Lyytinen SDR* (a codified rule), and a *Mintzberg SS/P-COL* device (a support-staff liaison). The "stepping stone" is the *unified object* of the three theories.

### 4.3 The operating core's professional pull

Four of five interviewees describe a *professionalization pull* from the operating core:
- **Niels:** "We create higher standards than what management would demand."
- **Kasper:** "The trust is on the team. There is no micromanagement."
- **Eva-Maria:** "I think if it's a good sprint for me, I think it's a sprint where the developers are content and have had a good sprint."
- **Birgitte:** "Now it's not the business who is in a position to say, 'You have to do this.' Now I can say, 'I would love to do this, but you have to tell me why.'"

This is *Mintzberg P-PRO*, *Lyytinen PN*, and *Weinberg DEAL-EA* rolled into one. The operating core *owns* the explanation; it does not blame external conditions. The professionalization pull is the *most consistently evidenced* structural pattern in the data.

### 4.4 The middle line as the principal resistance

Three of five interviewees describe *middle-line resistance* explicitly:
- **Niels:** "Middle management is maybe the hardest place to change."
- **Eva-Maria:** "I would like for the business to work in a more Agile way."
- **Birgitte:** "I would move heaven and earth to get that done. And I sometimes make decisions and then I have to say 'I'm sorry' afterwards to a leader of any kind."
- **Rasmus:** "Frequent shifts in leadership caused instability and changing priorities."

This is *Mintzberg ML* in its purest form: a part of the organization that *should* translate the apex's vision downward, but is itself caught between the old (command-and-control) and the new (servant-leadership) frames. The paper's *incompleteness* argument hinges on this pattern.

## 5. Counter-Evidence and Ambiguities

Following the quality criterion of *counter-evidence* (Section 4 of the appendix plan):

- **DEAL-DL is absent in every interviewee's first-person voice.** This is a notable finding, not noise. The only trace is the *inferred* DEAL-DL of the *removed manager* (Niels's account). The data does not contain a *current* manager blaming difficulty or luck. This strengthens the paper's anti-overstructured claim, but it also means the claim rests on *interviewees' self-reports* — a positive-bias risk.
- **The IP sprint / "cheat" is the only clean PA evidence.** Niels mentions the IP sprint, Eva-Maria describes her "cheat." No other interviewee names parallel ambidexterity explicitly. The paper should not over-claim this routine.
- **Anti-Modularization is the most unevenly covered fallacy.** Niels provides the anchor; Birgitte provides a second case (the TPM). Kasper, Eva-Maria, and Rasmus do not address it directly. The paper should note that the rejection of the Modularization Fallacy is *concentrated* in two interviewees.
- **P-BAL is suppressed in Niels's data but present in Rasmus's.** Niels emphasizes *integration*; Rasmus acknowledges the Copenhagen culture's higher churn. The paper should report both.
- **Rasmus's data is the only one that addresses the cooperative-ownership apex constraint.** The paper's argument about the apex enabling the transformation should cite Rasmus, not Niels, for the structural reading.
- **Eva-Maria's account is the only non-advocate, non-IT-grounded voice.** Her data carries the strongest *recursion-antidote* ("guardrail") and the strongest *IT-business mismatch* critique. The paper should not lose her.

## 8. Limitations of the Re-Analysis Itself

1. **One of the five interviews (Rasmus) was not coded in the v1 set.** This v2 analysis is the *first* formal coding of his data. A second reader would have nothing to compare to.
2. **The PA code is the most subjective of the five hyper-learning codes.** Borderline cases (e.g. Kasper's "leeway" in the QBR model) were *not* coded as PA. A second reader might disagree.
3. **The Mintzberg *pull* codes were added in the appendix plan but were not in the original v1 analysis.** This v2 coding is the first to apply them systematically.
4. **Coverage is high but not total.** DEAL-DL is the only code at 0/5. P-BAL is at 1/5 (with one explicit *resistance* in Niels). The paper should not over-claim universality.
