# Summary

## Introduction and Interview Setup

The meeting begins with Student welcoming attendees and confirming the setup for interviews, including the seating arrangements for six rows and the recording equipment. Student expresses anticipation for the interviews and wishes everyone happy interviewing.

## Role and Responsibilities in Cyber and Information Security

The discussion shifts to Simon’s role within the company, specifically in relation to the development practice. Simon clarifies that their role encompasses both cybersecurity and information security, which involves protecting secret documents and ensuring information is only accessible to authorized personnel on a need-to-know basis. The tasks are varied, often including ad hoc requests from employees with security concerns who are unsure who to contact. Simon’s department is responsible for handling these requests and directing them to the appropriate qualified individual. Simon acts as a bridge between security and development, as well as security and business owners.

## Interaction with Business Areas and Security Integration

Simon explains how they interact with different business areas within Nordlys, emphasizing that security is a high priority due to the company’s role in delivering critical infrastructure. They aim to integrate security early in the development process (“shift left”) to avoid late-stage issues. The intended method for reaching out to their department is through a ticketing solution, although tasks are not always received this way. Simon highlights the importance of being aware of business activities to advise on strategic security directions, especially when new products are being considered. Simon also mentions a model for delivering software within Nordlys, showing different business areas and the overarching importance of security.

## Departmental Structure and Project Management

The conversation touches upon the management of multiple projects within the security department. Simon acknowledges that this is an ongoing challenge as the department is relatively young, having celebrated its first anniversary recently. Simon, with a computer science background, focuses on development processes and interacts with product teams and other roles in the delivery model. Simon expresses a desire for teams to engage with security early in the project lifecycle, ideally before projects are finalized, to ensure security considerations are addressed proactively.

## Risk-Based Security Approach and Legal/Moral Obligations

Simon elaborates on the risk-based approach to security, emphasizing the need to identify and manage risks within projects. This includes legal compliance, especially given Nordlys’s role in delivering critical infrastructure for the Danish population, and avoiding significant customer loss due to data breaches. Simon mentions that there are specific points in the delivery model where roles reach out to ensure security is covered.

## Company History and Security Evolution

Simon discusses Nordlys’s history as a result of mergers, leading to diverse past practices in handling security. While the current Cyber and Information Security Department is young, security itself is not a new concern. Simon explains that prior to the current department’s formation, entities like Telia, acquired by Nordlys, had their own security departments and processes. The current effort involves merging these disparate security practices into a unified approach for the company.

## Decision-Making and Policy Implementation

The discussion addresses how decisions are made regarding security policies when different opinions exist. Simon states that strategic direction is set, and everyone works towards it. Simon emphasizes that managers rely on information from specialists they hire. When making decisions, managers consider the input from their teams to ensure they are leveraging the expertise of their employees.

## Predefined Security Policies and Compliance

Simon confirms that their department publishes security development guidelines and principles for Nordlys. These guidelines are broad to accommodate various scenarios and ensure compliance with laws. Below the legal compliance layer is risk handling, which involves managing customer data and avoiding reputational damage. Simon also mentions standardized solutions to avoid reinventing the wheel and the role of an internal developer platform team in providing detailed guidance on specific technologies.

## Ensuring Adherence to Security Policies

The conversation turns to how management ensures teams follow security policies. Simon explains that the process for inspecting work is still being built. Within the risk management process, if a product doesn’t adhere to standards, it’s flagged as high risk, and management must make a decision. Currently, there isn’t an automatic way to handle this; concerns are raised by individuals, and the security department works to mitigate the identified risks. When faced with a conflict between project deadlines and security, the decision ultimately rests with management, who are informed of the risks by the security department.

## Customer Requests and Risk Assessment

The scenario of a customer requesting a feature that might impact security is discussed. Simon reiterates that in such cases, the department identifies the risks, and management makes the final decision. Simon highlights the need to weigh potential profits against potential losses if something goes wrong. The profitability of a business today is intrinsically linked to its security, as losing customers due to security breaches can severely impact profitability.

## Escalation and Management Decisions

Simon explains that while management’s goal is a profitable business, this also necessitates a secure business. Conflicts can arise when specific departments have goals that might compromise security. In such instances, the security department engages with management to discuss security implications before proceeding. This process involves escalating issues, reaching a decision, and then moving forward.

## Challenges in the Security Role

The most difficult aspect of Simon’s role is identified as being “in all the places at once.” As the delivery model is still being established across Nordlys, and while strategic goals are in place, there’s a period where security needs to be integrated. Some processes are currently ad hoc but are moving towards formalization, which is a longer-term endeavor.

## Quarterly Meetings and Relationship Building

Simon describes the role in quarterly meetings (Q-planning) as building relationships with different teams. This involves making sure teams know who they are, what they can discuss, and what the security department’s priorities are. The aim is to encourage teams to reach out for guidance early in their work. Simon emphasizes that the role is to provide guidance, not to simply say “no” to development.

## Communicating Security Risks to Business Stakeholders

The discussion focuses on ensuring security risks are understood at a business level, not just a technical one. Simon explains that the risk team identifies risks within projects and assets, and they can leverage technical experts when needed. A structured, formal process for escalating these risks to a management board for decision-making is desired, though currently, this is handled on an ad hoc basis.

## Examples of Security Risks and Assessment

When asked for an example of a security risk, Simon mentions technical risks such as using outdated libraries or frameworks, and physical security risks like malfunctioning logs that could lead to office break-ins and theft of sensitive documents. Simon emphasizes that risks can be varied.

## Regular Checkups and Process Adherence

Simon explains that the frequency of regular checkups depends on how projects progress through the development process. Currently, it relies on someone reaching out to the department. Due to the large number of projects, a structured review of every project is not feasible. The goal is to establish a risk level for all projects and then focus on the most critical ones.

## Accountability and Policy Enforcement

The question of accountability arises if a project deviates from security policies. Simon states that if management has made a decision and it’s not followed, it becomes an issue to be addressed with the responsible manager. Simon clarifies that global policies are in place for all projects, and custom policies for individual projects are not created. The responsibility for deviations from global policies is a management concern.

## Handling Project Delays and Security Prioritization

When a project runs late, Simon clarifies that decisions about stopping or continuing are typically made by the technical staff working on the project, as the department doesn’t have a direct opinion on project timelines. Regarding prioritizing between security requirements and business needs, Simon explains that this is part of the risk-based process. The team assesses the risks associated with prioritizing business requirements and determines if those risks are acceptable and legal. For unclear solutions, teams are responsible for developing a design, which is then reviewed by the security department for potential risks and alignment with risk tolerance.

## Defining Security Success and Evaluating Solutions

Simon defines success in security terms as lowering the risk of any given project to an acceptable level. This means all identified risks within a project are within tolerance. When introducing a new security system, evaluation involves methods like penetration testing and retesting after implementation to confirm that issues have been resolved. The specific evaluation method depends on the nature of the solution.

## Essential Skills for Security Officers

Beyond coding and technical expertise, Simon believes understanding the inner workings of the business is a crucial skill for a security officer. This is because the department has technical staff who are experts in their fields. The security team’s role is to make connections within the business and facilitate change by knowing people and understanding how to influence them.

## Evolving Security Policies and Best Practices

The discussion addresses situations where teams follow best practices but a security risk is still identified. Simon explains that policies are updated based on various factors, including changes in best practices, test findings, or learnings from development teams. These policies are updated regularly to reflect current best practices, typically on a quarterly basis rather than monthly.

## Security Responsibility within Agile Teams

In an agile setup, Simon clarifies that there isn’t a specific requirement for a designated security officer on each individual team. The technical product manager is often responsible for ensuring security is integrated into features. If something goes wrong and it hasn’t reached the management level, a root cause analysis is performed to understand how and why it happened. The handling of such situations is then a matter between the responsible individuals and their managers.

## Review and Change of Security Strategy

Simon states that the overall security strategy was established shortly before joining the company in September of the previous year, and no new strategy has been deployed since then.

## Time Allocation and Stakeholder Engagement

Simon’s time allocation between developers and management depends on the specific part of the organization. Simon is aware of stakeholders who can effect change. In some areas, there is a need to engage with independent technical teams, while in others, decisions are more management-driven.

## Prioritizing Security vs. Business Needs

When conflicts arise between security requirements and business priorities, Simon emphasizes that the decision is entirely risk-based. The security team identifies and reports risks. If risks are outside their tolerance, management must decide whether to accept them or mitigate them. The level of risk determines the required chain of command for decision-making.

## Current Security Challenges and Past Problems

Simon mentions that there are ongoing security challenges, but they are not easily summarized for a general audience. The biggest challenge has been integrating security consistently into the processes of a large organization. The department has worked on building connections between people in the development process, which is an ongoing effort, but there has been some success in breaking down departmental silos.

## Security Flaws in Acquired Companies

Regarding security flaws in acquired companies, Simon identifies outdated components as the most common issue. These are often systems that have been running for a long time without significant updates or attention. Addressing these can be very difficult, sometimes to the point where starting from scratch is more feasible. The technical staff evaluates the situation and proposes solutions to achieve a tolerable risk level.

## Priorities in Company Acquisitions

Simon, having joined in September, feels unqualified to definitively state the top technical priorities for company acquisitions. Simon notes that acquisition problems often involve physical assets like servers or locations, which are not directly under the development team’s purview.

## Future Role Evolution with AI Technologies

Simon believes AI will significantly aid in managing the vast amount of knowledge within the organization and improve efficiency in reporting and information sharing. For the cyber and information security department, AI presents both opportunities and risks, particularly in using AI to test systems. The department is learning from the company’s AI department and aims to spread AI knowledge and tools throughout Nordlys, not just within security.

## Spreading AI Knowledge and Mitigating Risks

Simon discusses Nordlys’s AI department, which builds AI products and possesses valuable learnings. The goal is to disseminate this knowledge and the tools used by these specialized teams and the AI department throughout the rest of Nordlys, extending beyond just security to other areas. A key concern raised is the business use of company data to train models via external APIs. Simon acknowledges the risks associated with this, particularly regarding where data sent to Large Language Models (LLMs) goes. The focus is on developing solutions that enable desired functionalities while minimizing risks to an acceptable level, including exploring hosting options for models, such as regular cloud subscriptions versus self-hosting.

## Using Company Data for AI and Associated Risks

The use of company data to train AI models and external APIs is discussed. Simon acknowledges that building an AI department involves risks, including concerns about where data sent to large language models (LLMs) is processed.

## Misconceptions About the Security Role

Student asks what is often misunderstood about Simon’s role. Simon states that the biggest misconception is that the department goes around telling people “no” and stopping projects, which is not the job, though Simon admits they would like that authority. Student inquires about Simon’s participation in discussions for new features, customer meetings, and kickoff meetings to identify risks in future implementations. Simon confirms involvement through the product delivery model, which includes processes like system design authority and enterprise architecture, as well as ad hoc requests to the department. For larger transformation projects originating within Nordlys, Simon emphasizes the importance of early involvement to ensure interests are aligned.

## Challenges and Adaptability in the Security Role

When asked about the most difficult aspect of the role, Simon identifies “people” as the primary challenge, noting that while there is a software engineering background and strong development skills, dealing with people is something that had to be learned and improved upon. Simon also touches on the broad nature of the role, stating that it’s about adapting to situations rather than solely applying prior knowledge, reacting to market changes, new threats, and external factors. Simon acknowledges the need to follow processes but also to adapt those processes when they prove insufficient for handling external changes. Student asks about the inspiration for moving from software engineering to security, and Simon explains that the enjoyment of development stems from creating quality products, where security is a crucial component. Simon finds working in security within a large Danish company with critical infrastructure particularly exciting given the current global landscape.

## Security as an Integral Part of Development

Simon views cybersecurity not as separate from development but as something developers must address within their projects. Simon finds the solutions and designs required to mitigate current threats very exciting. Regarding benefits or drawbacks of being a security officer versus a developer, Simon believes it depends on individual preferences, such as a desire to interact with people and find the work engaging. For those aspiring to be security officers, Simon advises curiosity not only about technology but also about politics, both globally and within a company, and the ability to navigate these dynamics. When asked about areas needing improvement for project or organizational security, Simon explains that resources are finite, so efforts are prioritized based on risk-reward ratios, focusing on the most lacking areas.

## Integrating Security into Development Processes

Simon highlights work on integrating security more deeply into the development process as a significant focus. Other departments have engaged in different programs or sought influence elsewhere, depending on current priorities. When asked about progress in integrating security into the system, Simon describes it as a good start, acknowledging the difficulty in quantifying progress. Simon believes the necessary relationships have been established to ensure success and that the next step is to “push the wheel and then it start rolling.”
