Agile adoption, power dynamics, and decision authority

Interviewer: Let us start with the first question. It relates to the risks of adopting Agile. With regard to the adoption of Agile at Norlys, has it reshaped the power dynamics and decision authority in any way?

Niels: When you introduce a transformation, you can start by doing an organizational transformation, which always messes up, because you start by just putting people in a new box, and they will act as they did before. You need to transform the behavior as well.

So we actually started with behavior: trying to create a common agreement of what we are working on. By starting with changing the way we work, or the way people actually work, you make the basis for doing an organizational transformation afterwards. That reduces the risk of doing the transformation for the organization.

The first part we needed to do was to stop the bleeding. When you talk about Agile, it is nice to write about and read about, and teams are actually the easy ones. Management is the hard part, because they need to do things differently.

One of the risks we had up front was that we first needed to create transparency in a company where it did not exist. Different smaller silos were doing different things. There was a lot of value, but also a lot of delays, because we did not create value for a customer in a unified way. We did not have teams that did not wait for other teams. That means code starts to smell, interfaces change, customer needs change, and code is forgotten. This is the reality of what we needed to do.

So what we did first was not pretty. We started by getting people to coordinate around the task. After that, we created a list of what was prioritized. Then we went to the next step: getting an organization around this, so that this list was prioritized at the right level, based on the strategic themes that we had. But the steps are not finished yet. We are on the way.

Right now, we have an event that I am in charge of called Q Planning. We have a candidate list that is extracted from [unclear]. It is locked 14 days before. The teams have an innovation and planning sprint. They do their work, then we meet around it, and then we make the plan. That has reduced the amount of changes we do overall, even though the world still hits us.

So the risk is that if you just do the organizational change, people will fit into the pattern they had before. They will always do that. You need to change the organization, the teams, and the management. Management is the part that can mess everything up.

Management tensions in Agile adoption

Interviewer: You mentioned the adoption of Agile with regard to both teams and management. Let us dive deeper into the management part. What tensions in particular did you experience with the adoption of Agile in management?

Niels: Number one is that when you move up into the managerial layer, you reach more political ambitions. That means there are agendas you cannot see. There are power struggles. There are people who want to be the next CEO or the next part of that. But there is also the part of being used to delivering, and suddenly you are asked to do something different.

Luckily, we can start from the top. Our IT director, Christian Nord, actually has knowledge of what Agile is. In the first couple of runs, things were failing, and then part of the management was changed. They have done that quite a lot, which is not healthy for any company, but it is part of a hiring and firing strategy around this.

So on the managerial level, we are not finished. People are still changing positions and so on. The mindset up there is super hard to change. But they do have a grasp of knowing that when you prove that something works, they want to be in on it. So you need to prove what you do in parallel with working with them and explaining why it works.

If we go a bit further down, to line managers, their role has now changed. Usually they were the ones telling their people what to do, making the teams dependent on being told, and thereby not taking responsibility. That role needed to change.

We have educated our line managers. They are working together. Based on the first session, some of them felt that not everybody is going to stay here because of this, because we were charging them into a line where they were forced to become servant leaders.

So they are going from being an authority, the one deciding, the one who understood the business needs, to suddenly realizing: what is my new role? Am I actually a PM, or am I a line manager? This is super hard for people to realize. We tried to make them work with who they are, what they are doing, what they are not going to do in the future, and how they would change it.

I do not know what the results will be, because the education is new, but it has potential. This is also why we have Agile coaches. Scrum Masters are currently focused on the teams: protecting them and developing them. You also need somebody to work between the lines. That is where the Agile coach works with the different line managers, explaining that they are no longer in charge, protecting the teams, and working with how managers can enable their teams.

At the same time, we cannot lose business. So we do it in a row, so that we do not just tell them what to do. We need them to change into it. When they see the business take more responsibility, a manager who before knew how to deliver is also willing to let a bit more go. Things change as they see things work.

Interviewer: When you say that management people need to change, does that also mean that the teams need to be more enabled in terms of autonomy? You have all these middlemen trying to control everything, but the trade-off is exactly building the team, so they do take responsibility. Is that what I am hearing you say?

Niels: You have teams that did not take responsibility, or many of them did not. Some were used to taking even more responsibility than they do today. We need to break the curve, so that managers decide less and teams are given more responsibility, or at least given room to say: "We do not know what to do, who is going to tell us?" Then you can work with them and say: "You need to be proactive and ask for this. Who are your stakeholders?"

But it is small steps. We cannot just give them free rein, because they do not know what to do with it yet. Some teams are good at it. We can give more free rein there. Some teams need to be held in the hand a bit more, replacing who they had to decide before with somebody else telling them what to do.

So we sometimes put in things that do not make sense. For instance, the TPM role is an antipattern of huge proportions, but it is a stepping stone toward something better. Q Planning is [unclear] for all teams. Smaller reality is better, but you need to do something first. You need steps, to prove that it works. Then you can specialize, then people can mature, and management can mature.

Interviewer: So there is a big difference in how Agile the teams are?

Niels: Yes, huge. You realize that when you take a team or create a team from specialized people. One of the teams I am coaching needs to know about story points. Other teams are working with questions like: how are we going with our innovation and planning sprint? How do we make sure the team stays motivated? How are we connecting to the customers? How are we doing design sprints?

Some teams are further ahead. You need to look at what level they are on, where they are maturity-wise, where a change will give most value, and where you would cause the most damage by changing that first. We need to be aware that each change needs to give value, not just break things. Otherwise they need too long to catch up, and then they will again just fit into whatever box you told them to stay in.

Bottom-up, top-down, or middle-out?

Interviewer: With regard to the adoption of Agile principles, would you say it starts from the bottom up, from the top down, or from the middle out?

Niels: You need to do all of them. This is a terrible thing, because first you need upper management support. If you do not have that, it will fail.

Doing it bottom up will at some point hit a Tayloristic way of thinking. Companies are organized in a way that tells managers that they know everything. The problem is that with what we are doing, we work from something complicated into something complex. When you work in this domain, things start to get tricky, because the people actually doing the coding, if they are in contact with their business, can take huge steps and decisions here and now.

Creating a middle layer and a top layer makes decisions take longer and delays them. But this is how the old world looks, and how the world looks today. You can do like [James Cohen - unclear], who says fire all the middle managers and then things will work out.

Teams are actually not the biggest problem. Most teams will naturally, with how they work, diverge toward something Agile-ish, because things in Agile work naturally. Teams will somehow go in that direction, unless they are forced into projects by somebody above. They will find ways. Water finds ways, and this is what Agile found out. If something is natural, the structure stops it.

So you need upper management first. That is the first step. When they take the decision, then you need to work in both places. Middle management is maybe the hardest place to change. Teams can sometimes be delayed in doing it, because they will live in whatever you give them. I think you need to work on all different levels.

Agile practices as stepping stones

Interviewer: With regard to the current state of Agile, it sounds like it is going forward, but still somewhere in between. Is there any Agile practice that you continue at this stage that does not really provide value in itself, but prepares for future Agile practices?

Niels: When you say Agile, do you mean Agile, or do you mean Scrum, Kanban, Lean, Crystal? Agile is a way of thinking.

Interviewer: Let us keep it to the Scrum paradigm, because you use SAFe, right?

Niels: I think we went from a SAFe-ish way of working. The problem with SAFe is that it is a product you buy. It is not natural. It is a product. If you implement it, you get a feature factory, and that is not the point. That is not where we want to go.

Feature factories are a step in the right direction, and that is where we are right now. Transparency is key, and that is also in SAFe. Many of the roles and how we work with it are SAFe-ish. But when you go to product teams, which is a further step in the direction, you are outside SAFe and outside Scrum. Then you also discuss whether it makes sense to have a product owner. In productized teams, it is very different. Do you have a Scrum Master? They do not quite know, because it is more champions of Agile.

The ideologies you work with need to adapt to real life. That means we will always be somewhere in between. The rituals we take in need to make sense. Some teams are currently doing retrospectives. That is a super powerful tool. Some teams are not, because they are not ready yet.

Q Planning is, from my point of view, a necessity where we are right now. But it is not something I want in the future. When we get further, it needs to be in a different shape and format. So currently, we are not adhering to one fixed model. We are moving.

This is where theory and practice are quite different. You can take revolutions, as [James Cohen - unclear] would tell you about, or you can take evolutions, which is what we are doing here. Evolutions have less risk because they still allow you to deliver value during the transition. A revolution risks that you die because you did not succeed in your revolution. So I would say this is an evolution, not a revolution.

Concrete cases of Agile adoption

Interviewer: We are ready for the last 15 minutes. We spoke briefly about concrete cases. Our opening question is: can you remember any cases where a team could not embrace or adopt some of the Agile work methodologies you have implemented?

Niels: Recently, I had to present something from finance: a new finance model where they asked that every story, before it is closed, should have story points and an application connected to it, so they could know cost.

This was not something everybody liked, because some were estimating in hours. I had a one-and-a-half-hour talk with a Scrum Master about story points. They were not forced to estimate in story points. They were forced to translate whatever they had into story points at the end, so finance had a number.

This guy had an error rate on hours of less than six percent, which is pretty good. I told him: of course you can stay with how you want to estimate, because that is not where you will win the most. But you still need to do this, otherwise we do not know what things cost. Finance has asked about this.

Adding story points at the end is not Agile methodology. But having the talk with this guy resulted in him writing to me less than a week later: "Now all my three teams are estimating in story points. They could not find any poker planning cards, so we created our own."

Later, some of his team came to him about support tasks, errors in production, and maintenance. He said: "I just took it directly, created a task, and added story points in our estimation. Is it not a good idea that we do it like that?"

So because the team was forced to do something somewhere else, they had the need to rethink whether they would also use it for estimation. When you have a talk where you set people free to do it however they want, you create a lot of thoughts and let them settle. Sometimes they settle on something really good. In this case, they adapted a methodology for estimating, even though they were not asked to.

I have another team up here that was not originally a team. They were a department. They had architects and maintenance people who were only sitting and doing concrete maintenance, changing data, and so on. It was a great mix of people, but they were individualists. They knew how to do things themselves and never depended on others.

We took them, matched them into a team, and added a project leader who was now a Scrum Master. That is one of my success stories. They are not finished yet, and there are many things wrong, but the first step was that they actually embraced that they were individualists. We taught them what they were facing, and they went through the first forming phase: "Oh, we can actually see something on our board. Our tasks are up there. What does it mean when something new comes in?"

Just visualizing their work was the first step. The Scrum Master is a super intelligent woman. I had talks with her about what this is, how it is different, and when she needs to review how she does project leading and how she does this. Those talks made her move.

They got an internal product owner in the team. Not one who knows their stuff enough yet, but she is taking charge and taking initiatives. Their architects were used to delivering six meters of architecture and then leaving it or developing it. Now they are challenged to put their work on the board and estimate it.

They use T-shirt sizing. I had a discussion with somebody who had tasks, and I asked: "Is it an epic? Is it a story? Where are we? Is it a feature?" He said: "I do not know how big it is." I was trying to find a way to communicate, and their product owner sat on the other side of the table and said over the computer: "Is it a large?" He said: "Ah, yes, it is a large."

Then I asked him: "Can a large be done in 14 days?" He said no. Then it is not a story. "Can it be done in a quarter?" Yes. Then you are probably in the range of a feature.

It was just to give them a language. It was not a perfect understanding of everything behind how we do things, but it was enough for them to start working with it. They are doing retrospectives and using them. They are not mature, but they are a success, bringing together people who were individualists. They are now sharing workload in different places and understanding why things cannot just go into the team.

The product owner had some projects she converted into JIRA tasks, so the team now had them as tasks. In the beginning, she thought the product owner should not have anything to do with those tasks because they belonged to the project owners. I asked: "If the product owner outside the team came and told Emma that, what would you say as a Scrum Master?" Realizing how she worked, she changed her way and educated herself. This is where things go well.

Another place where things went very well in the beginning was our platform teams. They started as one team with a Scrum Master and product owner, and then they evolved. Now they are four teams, each with their own area.

Then a new manager came in and fired all the Scrum Masters, and things stopped. They created a new application called the [Guba Super Tool - unclear], but they did not know what their customers wanted, what people needed in it, or how to obtain that knowledge.

Things stop when you do not have anybody to kickstart the evolution. That manager has now been removed, which I am happy about. This is not official wording, but now they are going to have Scrum Masters again. They saw that it simply costs too much to remove the catalyst in the teams.

You do not necessarily need Scrum Masters as a title. You need champions of Agile who continue when things get hard. They still do retrospectives to make sure they evolve. They still make you estimate relatively, even though it is hard and people ask for hours. They do the work and keep the focus.

Developers will develop. They love to develop, and they love Agile when they do not need to fight for it. But when you need to fight for things, you divert to what means the most to you. Is it coding, or is it fighting for an Agile principle?

Management can mess up a lot. We are working with product lines, but nobody has product lines yet. Some managers had a clear view in their head of what a product line looks like. What is the product line? It is not a product line yet; it is an organization. Sometimes we fight to educate or delay management in their efforts, so that they reach a realization about things. Management is the strongest tool we have. If we enable it, it can contaminate the rest.

Story points and management metrics

Interviewer: You mentioned story points. How has the reception of such metrics been at management level?

Niels: When you talk about story points, people behave in different ways. Some teams start by saying: "We have an estimate of how many story points an hour is." They start somewhere. When they work with it for a while, they realize that relative estimation is something different. When they reach the place where you can challenge that, they will take it up.

Funny enough, we recently had a team where the developers actually wanted to talk about it and embraced it. The Scrum Master was quite surprised.

At management level, it is very hard to understand complexity when you are used to talking about delivery. You will still have people where you need to translate story points into days and say: "Currently, our team has this approximate delivery rate of story points."

My team had 89 story points in [unclear]. When my manager came and asked how many days that was approximately, I looked at my current translation. She knew that it was changing over time, which meant that the value of story points in days would differ as we worked, because complexity did not change, but the time it took us to deliver a story point changed. That meant that the cost was different.

You still sometimes need to adapt and talk in a language that people understand if they do not yet speak this language. When they get far enough, they will understand. They will try to see what story points are, and then it becomes a metric like anything else. They do not need days; they need a number that is understandable for them. If they know this team delivers this many story points, then they can estimate how much they get in a quarter. That is part of knowing your budgets.

It becomes harder when you talk value. That is much harder. We are not there yet. We do not talk value yet. Value is key for Agile thinking.

Closing reflections: theory, practice, and value

Interviewer: We are in the final closing minutes of the interview. We have about three minutes left. Is there anything you would like to add to the things we have talked about, anything you find particularly interesting?

Niels: If I look at the world where you are, you are working with theory, and practice is a lot dirtier, both when you code and when you do transformations. You can be a purist. The purists are the ones who travel from one company to another changing the structure. But changing the behavior is much more difficult, because you are working with people.

A developer will take the ways that work for a developer, because that is in their backpack. When you go from theory into this, remember: whenever you try to do this, have patience. The people in front of you are not just changing an idea or how they work. They are changing how they look at how to work. That is much more different.

If you only saw the shadows on the wall, you do not know that there is a world outside. This is what you are working with. It is psychology. Developers are as different as people can be.

That is one of the key takeaways from my work: theory is super important, but let it hit the real world, and let the real world have a veto right.

Remember that when you do transformations, you do them to create value. If you do not create value, then your transformation is worth nothing. Sometimes you need to take costs, yes, but you need to measure your value. Ask: am I breaking something that is currently creating value for the company? If yes, is it more important to do something else? Select where to start. Prioritize as you do your development tasks.

Norlys strategy and integration

Interviewer: What do you think about the One 2027 strategy?

Niels: The idea is nice. What is intended is not fully realized, but there is something more important with the One 2027 strategy. You took an entire company outside that has a totally different way of working, [C-Telia / Telia - unclear], and introduced it into Norlys, with [Myrna - unclear] as a partner. That is three different ways of working.

Right now, the first step I can see is that some of the borders have been wiped away. That means the baseline, not the wording but the intent, is being fulfilled. We are starting to work as one.

This is not the entire world. There is an infrastructure in telco that is not part of our world yet. There is a business that wants to be part of this. People are seeking toward it like moths to a flame. Will we succeed by 2027? In different formats. But the overall strategy of moving toward this is something where I think the intent is already really fine, because we prioritize as one.

Interviewer: A thought could be: what if you just embraced the different ways the departments are working? You took some kind of telco company that worked, they have been optimizing that for a while and are good at it, and then you put it together. Why not just let them do their thing? Would that be a bad idea? I am just trying to mix it up a little.

Niels: I think it would have been a different strategy, and it would have created two sub-companies. The power that telco brings would not have moved Norlys in the way Norlys needed to be moved, because the teams and knowledge were different. The managers were deciding. That was very different from what Telco or Telia was used to.

We started out by separating them into two bubbles when we prioritized. Then we found out that the connections became more and more frequent between the parts. That meant finding a uniform way for them to relate to each other needed to make sense.

If they want to create value from the synergy of covering telecommunication, energy, streaming, and entertainment, they have something unique. It is a combination that could become a potential heavyweight on the market that nobody would be able to compete with. You mix something that does not mix, but it can become something really great.

If you do not take advantage of it, and if you do not find out how to connect it, you will have separate venues and just sell products together. Then you do not realize the full potential of having a development muscle coming in from Telia with management that is quite different from Norlys.

Norlys has a way of working that is owned by shareholders who are not shareholders in stock, but customers. It is customer-owned. That gives a totally different way of viewing the world than a Telia way, where you just needed a churn rate with intake higher than outgoing customers.

So you have something stale in how they work and how their business model is going to change, and you need to take that into a world owned by customers. If you keep connecting the two without transforming it, Telia would not make money in the long run. That was why they were leaving the Danish market.

If you do not mix it, then it is just a cost and revenue question. But mixing it, from my point of view, creates a lot of trouble. In the long run, it will be worth it. We will succeed in having one company. Where people come from and where people stay in the long run does not matter, but delivering across does matter.

Right now we have a huge project in energy, and we are very reliant on the power of development in telco to develop shared capabilities. There are so many things we share, and these things are the hard parts. A billing system: right now we have two billing systems. Could it be done in one? No, because reporting in energy is very different. But a customer interface: how could we do that together?

There are many ways in which I believe they did the right thing by mixing it. Do I believe that parts of the company should still work in their own way? Yes. We have teams and customers related to streaming services. They are not part of this. They are doing it their own way until they are ready to step into this.

Not everything is forced into one structure, because that would not create value yet. But when they are ready to be part of us, or rather when we are mature enough for them to step in, because they are ahead of us in some aspects, then they will continue. When other parts of the business are ready, they will also be introduced.

But staying with a format that acknowledged the differences and just let them work separately was costing money. It was dying in the wrong direction. That was why transformation toward Agile was needed.

And it is not a good thing to call it Agile. It is called product, because that is the new black. Agile is the baseline for this, and it always will be whenever you do things like this. But there is a new labeling, and this labeling does create value if you use it correctly.

Interviewer: You are not saying that because it is management fashion?

Niels: Right now management fashion is autonomous agents, and we are also hitting that with our management.

Interviewer: Your new AI silo?

Niels: Yes, we have a new AI silo that was created. That is perfectly fine, but most of these teams need to know how to work with Agile. How do you do that?
