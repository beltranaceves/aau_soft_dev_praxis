A typical day and interaction with developers

Interviewer: The first question is: if I were a new employee at the company and we followed you for a day, what would that day typically look like for you?

Kasper: A typical day for me right now is mostly meetings. We are doing a major migration on our building platform, so my days are taken up by meetings, and between meetings I do documentation, diagrams, and gap analysis, to make sure that the topics that fall into the team that I lead are clear and that they have something to work on. So it is both talking to architects about their plan and drawing up the actual solutions. Right now I do not have any time for development, but when it is not this busy, I do normal development as well. So depending on where the company is, it can be both.

Interviewer: The next question was about your daily interaction with developers. From what you said, I understand it is mostly through meetings?

Kasper: Yes. We have two days a week where we physically meet at the office, to make sure we have the same dynamic and actually talk to our co-workers. Other than that it is mostly me passing down tasks or things that need to be done. Then we join up in meetings where we try to describe the solutions we are working on, and those are put into Jira where the developers can pick up the tasks or stories. The way we work is that when you have finished a task or story, you put out a pull request to the group so everybody can look at it. We do that broadly so everyone can see what is going on. So: two days a week physically and three days a week by Teams.

Interviewer: What would you say is the most important task of the day? Is it the meetings, mostly?

Kasper: Right now, yes, it is meetings.

Interviewer: Is that particular to this period because of the migration, or is it a general expectation of the role?

Kasper: The expectation of the role is that we participate as early as it makes sense for the development teams. The company can have an idea, and we would rather get in early before they go too far into the details about how they want to fix it. We want to know what the problem is so we can try to solve it. So the earlier we get into the topics or problems they have, the better. My role is to try to help the company as early as possible with ideas, solutions, or pushback if what they want to do is perhaps not the best for Norlys in general. But depending on where we are in the process, it is also about making sure we are developing the right things, using the right tools, and the right solutions. The tech lead part is also part of it.

Technical crises and architectural decisions

Interviewer: Thinking back on your first major technical crisis or difficult architectural decision in this new leadership role, what happened? And how did you approach solving it differently than you would have as a senior developer?

Kasper: When you have been in the business for a long time, you gain knowledge about what works and what does not, what looks good in theory and what is practical to do. Some years ago we had problems with an external service in Denmark that provides address IDs, a basic thing. Just before Black Friday, that service stopped working, so we could not sell anything. That is a big problem when you live on selling things at Norlys. It was not our service that went down, but an external service partner. What we did was ask the company very quickly: the step that is failing in the sales process is a validation step. We can fix the sales flow and start selling again, but we will lose this validation. Are you okay with taking the risk that we can no longer validate that a person lives at the given address? It was a quick conversation with the company about accepting the risk, and then pretty quickly I changed the service that handles that validation and took it out of production. Before, I probably would have started looking into other ways or alternatives. Now I was just: okay, let us first get sales going, accept the risk, and then migrate properly in the background.

Interviewer: What has been the most technically challenging project you have worked on at Norlys in general?

Kasper: When I first started, I was part of a brand new team merging systems for an internal technician tool. Because Norlys was merging two large companies, we had to combine six different legacy systems into one brand new UI. There were a lot of integrations, legacy code, and old systems. Because the new system was not the master data source, we had to track where information came from and push updates back accurately. It was a massive four-year project with lots of integrations, though I left it halfway through to become a tech lead.

Interviewer: And what has been the most technically challenging project as a tech lead?

Kasper: After I became tech lead in my current team, that was when EV charging really started to grow in Denmark. Back then it was not that big. But because of the growth, they had started out with an old Excel sheet tracking customers and chargers, and it quickly became a very manual task to handle. So we built a system where instead of the spreadsheet, they could enter all the information properly. Then because it kept growing, we also needed to build an onboarding flow: when someone ordered a charger for their home, they would go through a process where a technician would come to their house, they would take pictures of the electrical board, mark where they wanted the charger placed, indicate how far it was from the incoming power and whether we needed to do any digging, and so on. All of that was tied together and integrated into our service partner  [Tier, unclear], who performs the actual installation. It was a big mix: we started in Excel, built something to cover that, but then they liked it a lot, so we had to expand it. The electric car market just kept growing, and we ended up integrating with third-party suppliers as well. It is almost always when you need to combine customer logic with external partner integrations that problems and questions arise. But challenges are fun. It is not fun doing the same thing over and over.

Interviewer: You mentioned merging two IT landscapes. Right now, are there specific projects where you are dealing with legacy systems from multiple acquisitions?

Kasper: Yes, that is exactly the current project. We are migrating a really old legacy billing system to a new Azure-based system called Kraken, and the old system is going end-of-service in two or three years, so we need to get out of it. It is an on-premises system and we are building things in Azure, so just getting everything to talk together securely is a major concern because it is customer data. We also have a lot of developers working on their own system while we work on the new system with an external partner. That is why all the meetings: you need to get into very specific details about exactly what you are doing and when. And also: are we just moving the entire customer base over, or are we using this as an opportunity for cleanup? Some of this data has been in the system for twenty years. Maybe it is incomplete, maybe it needs to be updated. So you are also trying to improve data quality as you migrate, so that all customer records are consistent. There are a lot of things to handle at the same time, but you need to do it to make sure customers arrive at the new system in the right way.

Quarterly Business Review (QBR) planning

Interviewer: In the presentations, they talked about the Quarterly Business Review meeting that happens every few months. Can you walk us through how you and your team prepare for it, and how it helps you align with other teams?

Kasper: Before the QBR planning meeting, the company or the business side comes up with what they see as the most important things for the next three months. They give us the problems they think we should solve: that could be a new product, updated views, better UI, internal improvements, or customer-facing things. They come up with a big list, and topics get assigned to us or to another team. Then we go through it and see how much of this we can do on our own, and where we are dependent on other teams to deliver something to us first. Our team is mostly a service team providing services for others. So if the self-service portal needs some new data, they ask us, and we go to the source systems, figure things out, and give them access. We go through the list and see where we are dependent on others, or where others are dependent on us. We check whether we actually have access to the data or whether we need to talk to the team that owns that data source. We also try to give rough estimates of how large a task is or how much time it will take. By the time we sit down for the QBR planning itself, we have done most of that groundwork. Then we have conversations with the other teams, because each team has their own backlog. We need to agree on timing: if you need this from us in two weeks, we need to start here. If another team needs to deliver to the self-service portal by August, then they need to finish their part here, and we need to deliver ours here. That way we figure out how everything fits together. If one team has too many things to do and cannot be ready in time, then it makes no sense for us to commit to a task that depends on them. That is also where the business needs to step in and prioritize. At the end of the day, there is a confidence vote for each team: how certain are we that we can deliver our part? If everybody agrees, that becomes the plan and the roadmap for the next three months.

Interviewer: So most of the prioritization for the next three months happens during that meeting?

Kasper: The preparations happen beforehand, depending on the size and complexity of the problems, so that we are as well-prepared as possible for the discussions on the day. There will always be something to talk through on the day itself: you thought you had a plan, and then you forgot something or there is an extra request. You try to figure out whether you can still do it within the estimate. But a lot happens beforehand, and the meeting is about reaching agreement on what we are delivering and the timeframe, with all the teams able to do their part in the right order.

Interviewer: If a task is something completely new that has never been done before, what is the confidence vote based on in that case?

Kasper: If it is brand new and we have never done it before, there is a lot of uncertainty, and we mark it accordingly. If we have had the task presented to us a month or so in advance, we have had time to look into it. For instance, an integration to a new third party: we can go through the API specifications, go through the implementation details, and get close to an estimate. But if we are still uncertain, that is a risk we need to raise. Maybe it is a startup without good documentation. When you have been a developer for some years, you can often ballpark things based on past experience. But if it is a total unknown, we cannot commit to it, and we say so. The company or the business can decide they want to proceed anyway, and then as a team we raise the flag, communicate the uncertainty, and it is the business that accepts the risk or not. Or they may decide to postpone it to the next QBR. But it is really important to raise those uncertainties, because in the end it is the business that decides what we build and when. They need to be told so they can make a proper choice. It has happened that we said we were not sure we could finish something in time, and then the team relying on us said the same, and it becomes a chain where everything needs to go right for the solution to be delivered.

Technical debt and breaking down tasks

Interviewer: Norlys emphasizes two- or three-day deliverables managed in individual team backlogs. As a tech lead, how do you break tasks down into these small deliverables to ensure architectural integrity and avoid technical debt?

Kasper: When we get a story in, we go through it in our scrum sessions where we discuss and estimate tasks. We have a rule that if something is estimated at eight, it is basically a picture, meaning it is too uncertain and not ready. It needs to be broken down into smaller pieces. You do not want someone sitting with one task for two months. Smaller tasks are also easier to help with and easier to track: you can see you are making progress. So we will break a large story into several smaller tasks that might still be part of the same feature, but each task has a clear definition of done, like: created an API, or implemented this particular price calculation logic. It can be a half-hour task or a two-day task, but it cannot stretch so far that it becomes hard to track what is going on inside it.

Regarding technical debt: you always try not to introduce new debt when you start a task, but sometimes you need temporary solutions where you knowingly incur debt because you know you will revisit it later. For instance, if the company wants something new and our current platform does not support it, we can make it work, but we have to tell the company that we are doing this and that we will need time later to clean it up. Normal, ongoing technical debt we try to handle regularly. We do not take out entire sprints for it, but we take out time to make sure our services have up-to-date packages, and that code is not on deprecated dependencies. Lately we have tried to introduce an automated service that goes through our NuGet packages and tells us which services have obsolete or deprecated things, or where there are security issues. We used to have a third-party checking the code for us, but now it is more built into the pipelines and the repository itself, including tools like GitHub's built-in checks. We try to automate this because otherwise it is just someone opening each project every month and doing updates manually. We also need to look at whether it is a minor update, where things will work the same way, or a major update where we need to make sure nothing breaks. If a major update breaks something, we need to communicate that to the business and ask for time to make it right. It is much better to handle these things steadily on the road than to face many major updates at once two years from now. The business always pushes for new features rather than maintenance, so you have to push back and explain that if we do not keep the platform current, it may be too late in two years when they want to do the new thing.

Interviewer: Can you elaborate a bit on the automation process?

Kasper: We base our code in Azure DevOps and are slowly migrating to GitHub, which has built-in functionality that checks your code for exposed secrets, keys, and security flaws in packages. With AI becoming more prominent, a lot of these checks can now be done automatically and are built into pipelines and repositories rather than sent to a third-party checker. For the NuGet packages, we have been trying to use a bot to automate those updates. That is not something the business side asks for. It is something we as developers want, because we want to minimize manual labor and spend time on the right things.

Interviewer: You mentioned AI. Can you elaborate on how developers at Norlys are integrating it into the workflow?

Kasper: First, the company had to make sure that the AI tools we use are closed, so that the code we put into them is not shared with the model or used for training. Keeping our code inside the company was an important first step. Then we have been slowly using GitHub Copilot in Visual Studio to review code, suggest optimizations, and handle routine scaffolding, like creating a new service with the standard create, update, delete operations. That kind of boilerplate is the same every time. For more complicated business logic that is very specific to the Danish energy market, you really cannot rely on AI for that yet. We have been doing it for about nine months, so we are still learning how much we can rely on it. But the way AI is moving, I think more of these processes will be automated even further. Right now we still need to do the complex logic ourselves.

Safety nets and deployment strategies

Interviewer: Given the critical nature of the energy grid, could you describe your safety nets and deployment strategies to prevent catastrophic failures during updates?

Kasper: The way we do it, we have a development branch and a testing branch. We have automatic tests for the code, so it will test that everything works as before, and if you are building something new, you write a test for that. When the automatic tests pass, it is deployed to our test environment where people test manually, for instance checking whether the full order flow still works end-to-end. We also have QA for our app. If we introduce something new, we talk to our cyber security team to check whether we are exposing data or whether our APIs are locked down correctly. We use Cloudflare and have used various tools to mitigate security risks. In terms of internal communication, over the last couple of years we have been moving toward services communicating via messages: we were on Service Bus and are now moving to Kafka. The idea is to decouple things so that if one service fails, the whole chain does not go down with it. We have development, test, pre-production, and production environments to make sure we are not releasing something that will break. For things that are almost impossible to test outside production, we still try to make sure there are tests there too. For bigger releases, we also do smoke tests and consult security if we are exposing new endpoints or storing data in a different way. When we have major deliveries or updates, we usually push them between eight and eleven at night to minimize load on the systems.

Interviewer: If there is a system failure on a regular day, with no update involved, what is your role in that?

Kasper: We have a lot of logging, and we have alerts and health checks on the services. If a service is functioning but failing in some part, the first thing we do is look at the call stack for the failure to figure out where it is coming from. Then we look at whether it is an internal problem or a third-party problem. If it is internal, it could be the data being processed, or an edge case we have not thought about. We look at what data is going through, figure out whether there is a corner case, and then try to deploy a fix to test that specific thing. If that works, we put it into production. For planned releases, we have a change request process where we document the rollback plan and the emergency plan upfront: roll back to a former version, or deploy a new fix, depending on how critical the service is.

In terms of who handles things first: we have a first-level support team. If a customer has a failure, a support ticket is created, and depending on severity it is escalated to the team that owns the service. If it is really critical, they call straight away. When a service is handed over to support, the support team gets a session where we walk them through a checklist: where to look in the logs, what the service does, what the normal failure patterns are. They handle it first unless it is something we catch ourselves via alerts during working hours.

Projects that are killed or stopped

Interviewer: During the presentation we were told that some projects get killed early in the process. Can you walk us through an experience of a project where that happened?

Kasper: Normally it happens like this: the business wants to create a new product, say a loyalty club for electric car owners where members get discounts across the Norlys portfolio. The technical side of our team sat down, drew some diagrams, discussed solutions with me, and we agreed on an approach. The estimate came to around 200 to 250 hours. We came back with that rough estimate. Because of the size, it needs to go through validation gates in the company to make sure we are spending time on the right things. The business came back and asked whether we could do this differently or this instead of that. We said okay, but that changes the entire estimate. We went back, drew new diagrams, thought about the integration differently, and delivered that to them. They said they would look into it and get back to us. We took things into our sprints and then they came back with: actually, we are going to do something completely different. So after about one and a half to two months of thinking out solutions, drawing up diagrams, coordinating with external partners about what they could provide and what we would need from them, it all ended with the business deciding they wanted something else entirely. That is also how the market works. For instance, they once wanted to release a new product before a competitor did something similar and told us we needed it in fourteen days. Before I could finish taking that in, they came back and said, actually, ten days. It can go very fast in both directions. You always have to leave room for fast solutions when the market is competitive. Sometimes you have to give a little ground on process principles, but it has to be a two-way thing: the business also has to know that we cannot always do this, because other teams are waiting for us to deliver something.

Interviewer: In that example, it sounds like the project was killed before implementation actually started. Was there ever a case where implementation had started and was then stopped?

Kasper: Yes, we were building a phased integration into an old fiber company system. We completed the first step and were halfway through the second part when they told us they did not want the last part anymore, because they wanted to do it with a different integration. The things we had built in preparation for that last part were wasted. But time to market is really valuable, so you should only build what is actually valuable. We finished the second part, which still needed to be done, and left the rest open in case the company came back to it. I just found out they are now actually looking into it again, three years later. So it was not totally wasted. But yes, you can be stopped mid-way and told to stop. It also happens with services that are being deprecated: you are told this service is stopping in six months, so stop developing new things in it, even if the business has requests for it. The decision is not ours to make. If we are really sure that something needs to be done before we stop, we communicate that, saying: we need to finish this corner before stopping, otherwise it will be left in a state that is not feasible. But services are swapped all the time.

Interviewer: In your experience, are IT projects killed mostly for technical or business reasons?

Kasper: Everything I have been part of has been business-driven. The business changes its mind, often because they see something new in the market they need to react to, or because it is a timing issue and they can only do one thing, so they go with whatever makes most commercial sense. In five and a half years at Norlys, it has always been the business that takes that decision. I have never been part of a project that was stopped purely for technical reasons. Those kinds of stops tend to happen before actual development starts, based on my experience. There have been projects where the scope just grew too large, and then you need to stop, go back, and rethink before putting in more effort. The project can then sit still for a couple of months until the new scope is defined or it becomes clear you need to build something completely different. I have heard of both happening, but I have not been part of those kinds of projects.

Technology choices

Interviewer: What are the criteria for choosing technologies for a project? Can you give us a recent example of how your team decided to use a new technology or tool, and how much of that choice is made by the developers?

Kasper: In Norlys, the chosen stack is C# .NET. That is a decided strategy, and everyone uses it. There are legacy systems in other languages, but those are either being decommissioned or rewritten in C#. We use microservices, which is also company policy, and we try to use message-based communication, Service Bus or Kafka, as the communication layer. What the developers get to decide is which IDE we use, and how we want to do things within that framework. Norlys does not really care about how we do it as long as we do it in the agreed tech stack. If we want to use a new NuGet package for something, that is a personal choice within the team. We have agreed on certain things within our team to make sure we do things consistently, but there is really no wrong answer as long as you can argue for it.

My role in that is: if the team wants to try something new, for instance they were using React and wanted to try Next.js, they tell me why and what the pros and cons are. The pro is that it looks like the next big thing. The con is we do not know much about it yet. We talk about it, I take it to the business, and we figure out whether we are willing to take the risk, and whether the developers who will be using it are genuinely interested in educating themselves. If they are, Norlys does not have a problem with it.

On top of that, we have platform teams that work on improving the developer experience. They maintain a reference framework project that any team can pull down and use, with all the agreed patterns already baked in: pipelines, deployment into our Kubernetes cluster, everything. You do not have to configure a Dockerfile or a pipeline. It was not always like this. When I started, we had to do everything ourselves. Norlys is slowly maturing as an IT company to make sure people spend time on what matters to them. If you want to work with Azure infrastructure, there is a place for you. If you are more of a developer, there is a place for you. You do not have to know everything, and it is not assumed that you do.

Continuous delivery and the QBR model

Interviewer: What has been the most frustrating technical bottleneck you have encountered using the continuous delivery model, and how do you feel it has been handled?

Kasper: Before the QBR planning model, it was more ad hoc: meetings, something needs to be done, you figure it out with the other teams and just do the work. You might have some visibility into what was coming, but not in a structured way. With the QBR, it is much clearer: the business has acknowledged that these are the most important things for the next three months, and it gives a much more stable view of the focus areas. That is a really good thing.

The not-so-good thing is that it becomes very rigid. As I mentioned before, if something comes up urgently and they say we need this now, the QBR model says that is just not possible. Some of the flexibility we had before is gone. Except for real emergencies where they can always overrule it, you cannot simply slot things in the way you could before. If you have a good idea that you think is really valuable but it depends on another team that has already committed to something else, you have to wait. The flexibility for the company and the developers has been reduced.

Overall I see it as a good thing, because it forces the business to think ahead and take action. If they do not prepare, they show up to the QBR with nothing, and they do not want that either. It is helping the developers by giving them clarity, and it is forcing the business to commit to priorities. As long as there is some leeway built in, I think the QBR is the right model. There has been talk of bringing back a bit of that flexibility from before. And that is also what I meant when I said you need to make room in your planning for some flexibility. Some teams have a lot of day-to-day maintenance and incidents; others are more purely building new things. Each team needs to allocate a certain amount of time for maintenance and unforeseen things, and that buffer should be built into the planning from the start.

Interviewer: If you could go back and change one big technical decision you have made, what would it be?

Kasper: Not to have gone with Next.js when we did.

Interviewer: Can you elaborate?

Kasper: We had been using React throughout. When the developers came to me and said they wanted to try Next.js, we talked about it, I took it to the company, we discussed it again. I was not sure the ecosystem around Next.js was mature enough at the time, but I was talked into it and we went ahead. It worked really well in many ways, but when we got to the part where we needed to do an Active Directory integration into it, the tools for that were not ready. We jumped in a bit prematurely. The platform was still maturing. If I could go back, I would have insisted on staying with our existing React framework for that project.

Interviewer: Have you rolled back to React?

Kasper: No, we are sticking with it. I am telling the developers they need to pay close attention to what is happening in Next.js development and make sure the tools around it are maturing, because what we have is not quite production-ready in every aspect. You really do not want to be that early in the lifecycle of something where you pick up all the bugs and teething problems of the early versions. And because so much is changing in Next.js right now, we need to stay on top of updates, upgrade as new versions come out, and treat this project as one that is actively accumulating technical debt in a way that the others are not. That would probably be my most recent bad decision.

Interviewer: When integrating new technologies for data-intensive tasks, how have you practically balanced the trade-offs between performance, cost, and maintainability?

Kasper: Normally I am a bit cautious about going into new things. I like to make sure what we use is reasonably proven. But the balance is also about time to market. If it is very urgent, we do it with the tools we know so we are not learning something new while under pressure. If it is a longer project, we can easily try something new. With Next.js, we had about a year, and there was enough time to learn on the way. Norlys does not object to being innovative; I think they trust us enough to know we can make these decisions.

For performance-heavy things, we usually do a proof of concept: simulate the workload with our current tools and see how it performs. If it is not good enough, we figure out whether there is another way: a different database, a database add-on, or whether the service needs to scale better and use more threads. But we always start with the standard approach and then look at the performance from there, both now and in one year when data volume increases. Then we have a conversation with the business about what is acceptable. Does the customer want to wait ten seconds for something to be calculated in the self-service portal, or does it need to be faster? Faster always means more cost, so it is ultimately a trade-off the business decides.

Interviewer: Has there been a case where you had to make major changes to an existing system to get the performance you needed?

Kasper: Yes. We had a large database of electric car charging consumption data, and it grew very fast. When customers wanted to see their consumption history, it took longer and longer to retrieve because the queries were slow. What we did was create a new database where we pre-calculated some of those figures so they did not have to be recalculated every time. That was a major performance improvement. We now pre-calculate everything for the previous day each day, so the most recent data is always readily available and older data is stored in a calculated form. It comes at the cost of more storage, but it was worth it because we could give customers their data much faster and it was simpler to expose in the API. That was a pretty big performance update.

Interviewer: In these trade-offs, who decides whether to prioritize performance or cost?

Kasper: It depends on who identifies the issue first. If the company comes to us and says something is too slow, then someone using the system has noticed, and we need to do something. Or we can spot it ourselves during development, see that the performance does not look good, and raise it. It also depends on whether we are building it fresh and seeing the performance as we go, or whether something has been running for many years and the data volume has simply outgrown the original design. If I spot it, I do something about it, and depending on how much time it will take, I ask the company for permission to spend time on it because it will improve the customer experience. Or if the company comes to us with a complaint, they are already involved. It can go either way.

From senior developer to tech lead

Interviewer: What is the biggest difference between being a senior developer and being a tech lead?

Kasper: There are two main differences. The first is responsibility: you are no longer just responsible for your code, but for ensuring the team's overall deliverables meet company standards. You also have to communicate the company's direction down to your developers. Second, as a senior developer, your focus is entirely on your technical domain. As a lead, you have much greater insight into the business side and upcoming priorities. My time used to be 80% coding and 20% meetings, but now it is 60% meetings and 40% coding. I enjoy drawing up architectural solutions, but the transition definitely shifts your focus from purely building to taking responsibility for what gets built

Closing questions

Interviewer: About the billing system you mentioned: is it true that it uses some kind of AI to improve performance or customer experience?

Kasper: We are still early in that journey, so I have not seen the full potential of it yet. What I have been shown is that they have some form of AI to improve the customer service experience. But I am only responsible for a part of this integration, focused on the creation of new orders and the billing aspect. Kraken is the data source; we push data into it and pull data out of it. How data is handled inside Kraken, I do not know in detail, but I would expect that data processing and privacy compliance are covered in the contract Norlys has with them, given that Kraken operates in multiple European countries.

Interviewer: How early in the process do you typically identify when a project is not going to be viable?

Kasper: Most of the time, the business comes to us with a good idea and asks how we would solve it. I like it when they bring us the problem, not the solution. Usually we go back and forth with the business, come back with a plan and an estimate, say 300 hours, and then they go and ask: what is the cost of not doing this? Is it ten extra clicks for the customer consultant, or is it something more significant? They estimate whether the savings justify the investment, and how long they plan to use whatever we build. For instance, it does not make sense to invest heavily in our current billing platform because we are already getting out of it. It has to be a significant enough pain point to justify the time and resources. So normally that is where things stop: the business works out that the improvement, in time or money, does not justify the effort. They can also ask whether we can do half of it and meet in the middle, fixing some of the pain but not all of it. And then sometimes it is regulatory: new rules for the energy or network market mean we need to be compliant with something new, and that can cause us to stop other projects in order to handle it. But it is almost never because of internal development complexity alone, at least in my experience.

Interviewer: Is being a technical developer more satisfying, or is the decision-making and leadership side more satisfying?

Kasper: I started out as a really technical person. As I got older, new things started to interest me more. When I was younger I read a lot about all the new tech coming out, but as I got more comfortable I started enjoying the problem-solving that happens before the code more. It has been a natural transition for me. I have team members who are still very technical and really do not want to deal with all the upfront figuring-out; they want to figure things out in the code. So I think it is a personal preference. I have enough years of experience as a core developer to know what works on a technical level, but I am also genuinely interested in the business side. This role covers both. I feel like I am slowly moving further from development and toward the role more fully, and at some point it will become hard to be a tech lead if you are no longer interested in tech. But I am not there yet, and I will figure out what to do when I get there.

Interviewer: The platform at Norlys is described as self-organizing and self-managing. How do you as a tech lead help your team be that way?

Kasper: There is a work side and a personal side. I am a pretty social person. I talk with other team members not just about work but about everyday things: getting to know each other, what they like, what their lives are like outside work. I believe that for a team to really be a team, you need to know a bit more than just work-related things. So we try to do team events where we do not talk about work at all: going out to eat, climbing, activities together, to help everyone understand each other better in a work environment as well. My role is to make sure everyone is heard. I check in with everyone about what they are doing and how it is going. If someone wanted to get involved in a service that two others were primarily building, they are welcome to. It does not matter who does what as long as we get things done. We organize as a team: tasks come in, and we figure out together how to handle them and who does them. Some tasks are more fun than others, and the less fun ones still need to get done, but as long as we can agree that those rotate, it works. I do not have any hiring or firing responsibility. That is the manager role. My role is more to make sure we are all walking in the same direction, using the same tools we have agreed on, and that people are happy. If someone is stuck, we figure out how to get them unstuck: swap who is working on a service, or find another way through. The trust is on the team. There is no micromanagement. It is: come to me if there is a problem. Otherwise you know best what to do. Six developers, a Scrum Master, and a product owner. That is my team.

We also try to make sure that knowledge is not concentrated in one person. When we build a new service, we make sure everyone knows what is going on with it: walk through the basics, maybe walk through some of the code. If someone did something in a new way, we bring it to our knowledge-sharing meetings. The team needs to organize themselves and they are left to do that by management, which is really nice, because then you can do it the way that actually works for the team rather than being forced into a box.
