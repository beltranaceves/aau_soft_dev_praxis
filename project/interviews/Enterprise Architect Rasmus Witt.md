1. Can you talk us through a typical work day or work week for you to get a better idea of what your job entails?
Response:
My work is very diverse. I’m the lead architect in a large program focused on implementing new billing and CRM solutions. I see my job as removing obstacles for teams. Early on, my focus was aligning the program with our target architecture—both within the energy domain and across the entire company, including Telco, to support our goal of being “One Norlys” toward customers.

That involved a lot of mapping, discussions with teams and vendors, and organizational decisions. Currently, my focus shifts week by week—for example, this week I’m deeply involved in resolving payment-related challenges so teams can move forward without blockers.

2. Would you say that most of your responsibilities entail coordinating both people and information between those people?
Response:
Yes, definitely—coordinating people and information, and providing guidance. It’s very people-oriented.

3. In an organization that almost never cancels projects, what does a failed project really look like? Has there been a recent example?
Response:
It’s hard to define failure because before something completely fails, you usually change direction. We have large programs where the approach has changed three or four times. The project may still be ongoing, but it looks very different than it did two years ago. The scope and execution change—so when exactly does it “fail”? That’s difficult to define.

4. What mechanisms exist to pivot like that? Are QBRs or planning sessions used, or something else?
Response:
For large projects, these decisions usually happen at the program level, especially in the steering group. Issues are escalated there, discussed, and fundamental changes are decided. The right people are involved, but it’s outside the normal delivery model.

5. What about urgent situations, like P1 incidents? Do you use war rooms?
Response:
It depends on the issue. For security incidents, you pull in the necessary experts and senior management. During the Log4j incident, for example, we set up war rooms, pulled people in during weekends, scanned repositories, and put everything else on hold to focus fully on the issue.

6. Is there a hiring strategy for technical roles? Who decides it, and how do values factor in?
Response:
Values are a core part of hiring. We assess whether people fit our values and culture. A job interview is just as much about whether we’re a good fit for the candidate as vice versa. If you’re not aligned with how we work, it likely won’t be a good match. Our values—like communality and responsibility—are fundamental.

7. How do you manage culture when merging companies where you can’t “hire for culture”?
Response:
In mergers, cultures merge too, and it takes time. For example, when we acquired Telia Denmark, their performance-driven culture mixed with Norlys’ existing culture. Over time, we’ve found a balance, but there were clashes initially.

8. Where does the dominant culture at Norlys come from?
Response:
It stems from the ownership structure—a cooperative model where around 800,000 households are owners. Representatives elected by these owners have final approval authority on major decisions, which strongly influences company culture.

9. Can you explain that cooperative structure in more detail?
Response:
The representatives can propose and influence decisions. While the CEO and board drive strategy, they must argue their case and get approval from this representative body. Nothing is guaranteed to pass.

10. During the presentation, you mentioned the “golden path.” Who defines it and who decides what’s best?
Response:
The responsibility lies with our platform teams, such as the integration platform team. From the EA side, we define high-level principles, but we involve solution architects and tech leads in shaping the details. Proposals are discussed, refined, and approved in our architecture studio with management support.

11. How do you onboard newly merged companies into these policies and guidelines?
Response:
We first determine which technical stacks to keep. We currently support both Java and C#. We define a preferred path for new development while still supporting existing expertise. It’s about balancing standardization with reality.

12. How do you encourage developers to follow these guidelines?
Response:
Through developer experience and friction. For example, using our Kubernetes platform is easy and self-service, while going off-platform requires much more effort and justification. Guidelines exist for security, observability, and operations. If you deviate, you must prove it’s cheaper, faster, and equally reliable.

13. So friction and developer experience are the main enforcement mechanisms?
Response:
Yes. Guidelines exist for a reason—security, telemetry, observability. If you choose another approach, you must meet the same standards.

14. What is the Net Promoter Score (NPS), and how is it calculated?
Response:
I don’t know how it’s calculated in detail. I see it as a measure of customer satisfaction. It’s one KPI among others.

15. How do you personally measure customer success?
Response:
I focus more on operational data—customer service reports, call reasons, and pain points. For example, if many calls relate to invoice confusion, that’s where we should focus improvements.

16. As an enterprise architect, what decisions are actually in your power to make?
Response:
Our power lies in architectural governance. Decisions flow through layers:

System Design Authority (SDA)

Enterprise Design Authority (EDA)

Architecture Steering Core (VP / CTO layer)

Depending on scope and cost, decisions move up these levels. We guide, approve, or escalate accordingly.

17. How do you avoid becoming a bottleneck?
Response:
Not everything goes through governance. Small, isolated changes can be handled by teams. Larger or cross-team impacts are escalated. We rely on close communication to catch issues early.

18. Is there a contradiction between vertical management and horizontal team autonomy?
Response:
No—it’s both. Teams coordinate horizontally during planning and execution, while priorities are set top‑down. It’s a hybrid model.

19. Have you experienced mandates coming directly from VP level that you had to manage?
Response:
Yes. As lead architect, I often have to balance conflicting strategies and initiatives, involve stakeholders, and navigate constraints while moving forward.

20. With such a high customer-to-employee ratio, are there values or techniques that support this?
Response:
It’s largely due to the industry. We’re not a budget brand—we focus on quality service and accessibility, which impacts how we operate.

21. Has the Copenhagen acquisition changed company culture?
Response:
Yes. There’s a more competitive culture and higher churn in Copenhagen compared to other regions. Big city dynamics play a role.

22. How much do company politics affect your daily work?
Response:
A lot. I have to balance domain needs with company-wide strategy. Large programs create political tension because shared solutions increase complexity and risk but align with strategic goals.

23. Can you share examples of big successes and less successful efforts?
Response:
Less successful: frequent shifts in leadership caused instability and changing priorities.
Successes: small empowered teams with strong mandates delivering complex projects in under a year—even if not perfect, it enabled fast execution.

24. How do you balance agile methods with long-term planning in infrastructure-heavy industries?
Response:
Infrastructure plans span 30–40 years. Customer-facing systems can move faster. We operate more like a software company on the customer side because we don’t manage as much physical hardware.

25. After pivots, do you analyze what went wrong?
Response:
Yes, usually within the project. But the process could be more structured. Often pivots happen quickly due to compliance or feasibility constraints.

26. Is knowledge effectively shared across teams?
Response:
Not enough. I’d like more guilds—forums where people share technical knowledge across teams.

27. How does AI fit into knowledge sharing?
Response:
We’re investing in AI agents, especially in customer service and development workflows—coding, reviewing, testing, and even architecture support.

28. Is AI a political topic internally?
Response:
Not really. Opinions differ. Some developers already rely heavily on AI agents. The discussion is ongoing, but adoption is growing.

29. Does EU regulation slow AI adoption?
Response:
Yes, especially as a critical infrastructure provider. Data locality, compliance, and training data create complexity. This space evolves extremely fast.

30. How did you move from a technical role to enterprise architecture?
Response:
I followed problems upward—from developer to tech lead to solution architect to EA. I kept encountering systemic issues that required broader influence to solve.

31. Is Norlys following standard organizational models or breaking the mold?
Response:
It’s mostly standard (similar to SAFe), adapted to our needs. Agile alone isn’t enough at this scale—structure is necessary.

32. Has AI fundamentally changed how you work yet?
Response:
Not yet. It’s improving existing processes, but true transformation requires governance and structure.

33. How mature is the company in adopting new processes?
Response:
We only adopt processes if they add value. If something doesn’t work, we change it. Criticism and improvement are encouraged.

34. Can you give an example of a big procedural change from feedback sessions?
Response:
Most changes are incremental. Big structural changes happen at senior management levels. Smaller improvements spread organically.

35. Is there a “developer advocate” role here?
Response:
Not as a single role. Platform teams, agile processes, and EAs collectively perform that function.

36. How open is communication across hierarchy?
Response:
Very open. The goal is a flat structure where you can speak directly to executives when appropriate.

37. How do you involve subject-matter experts in strategic decisions?
Response:
It depends on the domain. We have internal experts in billing, commercial products, utilities, etc., and we involve them as needed.

38. What’s the most technologically impressive part of the company?
Response:
The mobile core network—supporting millions of devices reliably. It’s impressive and very complex.

39. What do you enjoy most about your job?
Response:
The diversity, context switching, and even the chaos. I enjoy making order out of complexity.

40. How do you handle teams that don’t follow processes?
Response:
That’s primarily a management responsibility. Some teams follow better than others—it depends on leadership.

41. How do you balance process with human differences?
Response:
Through coaching and guidance. People solve problems differently, and that’s okay. The goal is value, not rigid adherence.

42. How do you support upskilling?
Response:
Through courses, education, mentorship, and manager support. It’s encouraged and budgeted.

43. How are technical workers evaluated?
Response:
Through peer feedback and people reviews, combined with manager assessment. There are no strict productivity metrics.

44. Isn’t peer evaluation biased?
Response:
Managers mitigate bias by context, ongoing feedback, and close collaboration. Nothing new should surface in reviews.

45. Do you use objective metrics for developer performance?
Response:
No. It’s too complex and misleading. Performance is better evaluated at the team level.

46. Is there a fundamental change you’d make if you could?
Response:
Yes. I’d restructure the organization around vertical ownership of customer journeys rather than separating customer, energy, and telco domains.

47. What limits observability the most today?
Response:
Lack of end‑to‑end ownership. If teams owned full customer journeys, success would be easier to measure and improve.

48. How long does it take to become useful in such a large company?
Response:
Not long—if work is well-scoped. Broader value comes over time as domain knowledge grows.
